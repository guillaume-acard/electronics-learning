#What is a microchip?

A microchip (an integrated circuit, or IC) is a whole circuit, made of transistors, resistors, diodes and sometimes capacitors, built onto a tiny slice of silicon and sealed in a plastic body with metal legs (pins).

Instead of soldering 30 parts to make a timer or an amplifier, you use one chip that already contains them. Everything you learned on the transistor, resistor and diode pages is inside, just microscopic.

  • Finding pin 1: look for the notch or dot. Pin 1 is next to it, and the pins are numbered anticlockwise (seen from above): down the left side, then up the right side.
  • Power first: every chip needs a + supply pin (VCC/VDD/VS) and a ground pin (GND/VSS). Get these wrong and the chip dies.
  • 100 nF beside the power pin: the decoupling capacitor from the capacitor page (example D).
  • The datasheet is the chip's instruction manual. Search "chip name datasheet" for the pinout, limits and example circuits.

#Chip names decoded: prefixes, families, suffixes

A chip name usually has three parts: a prefix (who makes it, or which family), a number (what it does) and a suffix (the package and grade). NE555P = made in the NE (Signetics) line · 555 timer · P = plastic DIP.

The number is what matters most: the same function is often made by several companies under different prefixes. An NE555, LM555 or SA555 are the same timer; a CD4017, HEF4017 or MC14017 are the same counter.

Suffixes: package and grade

  • N, P, PU, E, BE, CN: plastic DIP (through-hole, 2.54 mm): fits your breadboard. ✓
  • D, DR, M, SO, SOIC, TSSOP: surface-mount: tiny, needs an adapter board for breadboards.
  • C, I, A, M (in the middle or at the end): commercial / industrial / automotive / military temperature range.
  • B on 4000 chips (CD4017B): "buffered", the standard version today.
  • Special cases: LM35DZ: D = accuracy grade, Z = TO-92 package. L7805CV: C = commercial, V = TO-220.

Linear / analog

Work with smooth voltages: op-amps, comparators, audio amps, regulators, sensors.
Yours: LM358, LM386, LM3914, 7805, LM35.

⏱️

Mixed (analog + digital)

Analog inside, on/off outside: timers, converters, chargers.
Yours: NE555, TP4056.

🔢

Logic (digital)

Only 0 and 1: gates, counters, decoders, shift registers. Two families: 4000 (3–15 V) and 74HC (2–6 V).
Yours: CD4017, CD4511, CD4518, 74HC595.

🧠

Microcontrollers

Programmable: a tiny computer that can replace many logic chips with code.
Yours: ATmega328P (Arduino).

#Recap

  • A chip is a whole circuit (mostly transistors) in one package. Pin 1 is by the notch, numbered anticlockwise. Always connect power and add a 100 nF capacitor beside it.
  • NE555: fills and drains a capacitor between ⅓ and ⅔, which gives blinks, tones and delays.
  • 7805: an automatic valve that holds exactly 5 V, turning the extra into heat.
  • LM358: compares two voltages (yes/no) or amplifies precisely with feedback.
  • LM339: four comparators with open-collector outputs: each one pulls to ground when − is above +.
  • LM386: push-pull power to drive a speaker, with gain 20–200 (your Ruby amp).
  • CD4017: moves a single "on" along 10 outputs, one per clock pulse.
  • CD4511: turns a 4-bit number into the 7 segments of a digit.
  • CD4518: two decimal counters (0–9) that chain into 00–99.
  • CD4013: two 1-bit memories that copy D to Q on the clock edge; Q̅ → D makes a divide-by-2.
  • CD4060: a quartz oscillator plus 14 divide-by-2 stages: 32.768 kHz → 2 Hz.
  • 74HC595: shifts bits in one by one, then latches all 8 outputs at once.
  • LM3914: 10 comparators on a resistor ladder turn a voltage into a bar of LEDs.
  • L293D: two H-bridges: each motor end is switched to + or −, so the motor can turn both ways.
  • TP4056: charges a Li-ion cell with constant current, then exactly 4.2 V.
  • ATmega328P: a whole computer on a chip, the brain of the Arduino.
  • The LM35 temperature chip is on the Sensors page.
