1What is a microchip?

A microchip (an integrated circuit, or IC) is a whole circuit, made of transistors, resistors, diodes and sometimes capacitors, built onto a tiny slice of silicon and sealed in a plastic body with metal legs (pins).

Instead of soldering 30 parts to make a timer or an amplifier, you use one chip that already contains them. Everything you learned on the transistor, resistor and diode pages is inside, just microscopic.

  • Finding pin 1: look for the notch or dot. Pin 1 is next to it, and the pins are numbered anticlockwise (seen from above): down the left side, then up the right side.
  • Power first: every chip needs a + supply pin (VCC/VDD/VS) and a ground pin (GND/VSS). Get these wrong and the chip dies.
  • 100 nF beside the power pin: the decoupling capacitor from the capacitor page (example D).
  • The datasheet is the chip's instruction manual. Search "chip name datasheet" for the pinout, limits and example circuits.

3Chip names decoded: prefixes, families, suffixes

A chip name usually has three parts: a prefix (who makes it, or which family), a number (what it does) and a suffix (the package and grade). NE555P = made in the NE (Signetics) line · 555 timer · P = plastic DIP.

The number is what matters most: the same function is often made by several companies under different prefixes. An NE555, LM555 or SA555 are the same timer; a CD4017, HEF4017 or MC14017 are the same counter.

4NE555 timer: the blinker

What it does: it turns on and off by itself at a rate you choose with two resistors and a capacitor. Made in 1972, it's still produced by the billions.

How it works inside: a capacitor fills through R1 + R2. Two comparators (voltage watchers) watch it: when it reaches ⅔ of the supply, the chip flips its output off and opens a discharge transistor that drains the capacitor through R2. When it falls to ⅓, the output flips back on and filling starts again.

Water analogy: a tank with a float. When the water reaches the high mark, the float opens a drain. When it drops to the low mark, the drain closes and the tank refills. The light is on while the tank is filling.

Frequency = 1.44 ÷ ((R1 + 2×R2) × C). Pick 10 nF and the blinking becomes an audible tone: that's how cheap buzzers, sirens and the classic "Atari Punk Console" synth work.

57805 voltage regulator: the steady 5 V

What it does: you feed it anything from about 7 V to 20 V, and it always gives exactly 5 V out, even as the battery drains or the load changes. (Its cousins: 7809 → 9 V, 7812 → 12 V, and the LM317, which is adjustable.)

How it works inside: a big transistor acts as a valve between input and output. A reference (a precise Zener-like voltage) and a comparator constantly check the output: too high, close the valve a bit; too low, open it. The extra voltage is burned off as heat: power = (Vin − 5 V) × current.

Water analogy: a pressure-reducing valve on a house's water supply. Whatever the street pressure, the taps get the same gentle pressure, and the valve gets warm doing it.

Note the dropout: the input must be at least ~2 V above 5 V (so 7 V or more). Big voltage drop × big current = lots of heat, so add a heatsink. Without one, it protects itself by shutting down when too hot.

6LM358 op-amp: the comparer and amplifier

What it does: an op-amp (operational amplifier) has two inputs, + and −, and one output. It looks at the difference between the inputs and multiplies it by a huge number (about 100,000). The LM358 has two op-amps in one chip.

Two ways to use it:

  • Comparator (no feedback): the huge gain means the output simply slams to high if + is above −, or low if + is below −. It makes a yes/no decision: "is it dark?", "is it too hot?"
  • Amplifier (with feedback): two resistors feed part of the output back to the − input. The op-amp adjusts its output until both inputs are equal, which gives an exact, stable gain = 1 + Rf ÷ Rg.

Water analogy: a super-sensitive see-saw (the two inputs) connected to a powerful pump (the output). The slightest tilt drives the pump fully one way. With feedback, the pump's own pressure pushes the see-saw back level, so it settles at an exact amount.

7LM339 quad comparator: four yes/no questions

What it does: four comparators in one chip. Each one compares two voltages and answers one question: "is the − input higher than the + input?" If yes, its output connects to ground. If no, its output is simply disconnected (it "floats"). It runs from one supply, 2 to 36 V, and its inputs work right down to 0 V.

How it works inside: each comparator is a high-gain amplifier (like an op-amp) with no feedback: the tiniest difference between the inputs, a few millivolts, swings it fully one way or the other. Its output stage is a single transistor to ground, called an open collector. That is why every output needs a pull-up resistor to + (to give a clean "high" when the transistor is off), or an LED + resistor coming from + into the pin (it lights when the transistor switches on).

Water analogy: four lock-keepers, each watching two water levels. When the level on his − side gets higher than on his + side, he opens a drain to the ground. He never pumps water in, he can only drain it.

Move the signal (on −) past the threshold (on +). Then switch the pull-up off: without it, a "no" answer leaves the output floating and nothing defines the voltage.

8LM386 audio amplifier: drives a speaker

What it does: it takes a small audio signal and gives it enough power (up to ~0.5–1 W) to move a speaker cone. It's the heart of countless mini amps, including your Ruby amp.

How it works inside: an input stage, a gain stage, then a push-pull output: two transistors, one that pushes the speaker out (from +9 V) and one that pulls it in (to ground). The gain is 20× by default, and up to 200× if you connect a capacitor between pins 1 and 8. In the Ruby, the 1 kΩ gain knob sits there, so you choose anywhere in between.

Water analogy: two pumps facing a flexible membrane (the speaker cone). One pushes, the other pulls, in time with the music.

The output sits at half the supply (4.5 V) and swings up and down around it. That's why there's a big 220 µF capacitor before the speaker: it blocks the 4.5 V DC and passes the music, exactly the coupling trick from the capacitor page. Push the gain too far and the swing hits 0 V / 9 V and clips, which is the Ruby's overdrive.

9LM3914 bar-graph driver: a voltage becomes a bar

What it does: it lights 1 to 10 LEDs depending on a voltage: a little voltage lights the first LED, the full-scale voltage lights all ten. It's a ready-made "level meter": battery gauges, VU-meters, thermometers (project 21 builds the same thing by hand with 3 × LM339, and lists the LM3914 as a variant), no microcontroller needed. It also regulates the LED current itself, so no resistor per LED.

How it works inside: a ladder of 10 equal resistors between RLO and RHI makes 10 evenly spaced voltages. 10 comparators (op-amps, see the LM358) each compare the input with one rung: if the input is higher, that comparator turns its LED on. A built-in 1.25 V reference lets you set the scale with a trimmer, and the current it supplies (× 10) sets the LED brightness. MODE chooses a bar (all LEDs up to the level) or a single moving dot.

Water analogy: a tall tank with 10 overflow holes at equal heights, each feeding a little light. The higher the water, the more holes spill.

Cousins: the LM3915 (logarithmic steps of 3 dB, for audio) and LM3916 (VU-meter scale). Chain two LM3914s for 20 LEDs. Gotronic: LM3914N, code 21122.

10CD4017 decade counter: the LED chaser

What it does: it has 10 outputs. Only one is on at a time. Each pulse on the clock pin moves the "on" to the next output: 0, 1, 2 … 9, then back to 0.

How it works inside: a chain of flip-flops (tiny 1-bit memories made of transistors) counts the pulses, and a decoder turns the count into "light output number N". Connect an output to the reset pin and it starts over early, so it counts to any number from 2 to 10.

Water analogy: a rotating distributor valve that sends water to the next of 10 pipes each time you push its lever. The classic pairing: a 555 provides the clock pulses and the 4017 counts them.

11CD4511 decoder: number → digit

What it does: it reads a number from 0 to 9 written in binary on 4 wires (A = 1, B = 2, C = 4, D = 8: that's called BCD, binary-coded decimal), and lights the right segments of a 7-segment display. Give it 0110 (4 + 2 = 6) and it lights a, f, g, e, d, c: a "6". It also drives the segments itself, with up to ~25 mA each.

How it works inside: a small latch (a 4-bit memory) holds the number, then a decoder made of logic gates works out which of the 7 segments each number needs (a hard-wired lookup table, like the 27-diode matrix of dice project 14), and 7 output transistors push current into the segments. Three control pins: LT (lamp test: all on), BI (blank: all off) and LE (latch: freeze the digit).

Water analogy: a fountain with 7 nozzles shaped like a digit. You set 4 levers (on/off), and a mechanism behind the wall opens exactly the nozzles that spray the shape of that number.

Click the 4 input bits (or the controls) to see what the display shows. Numbers above 9 (1010–1111) are not valid BCD: the 4511 shows nothing.

Only for common-cathode displays like your SC39-11SURKWA (COM to ground). Each segment still needs its own resistor (470 Ω–1 kΩ at 5–9 V). Used in project 15, project 19 and Using real parts.

12CD4518 dual counter: counting in decimal

What it does: it contains two counters, A and B. Each one counts clock pulses from 0 to 9 and then starts again at 0, giving the count in BCD on 4 outputs (Q1 = 1, Q2 = 2, Q3 = 4, Q4 = 8): exactly what a CD4511 wants. Chain the two halves and you count from 00 to 99.

How it works inside: each counter is 4 flip-flops (1-bit memories) that count in binary, plus a little logic that jumps from 1001 (9) straight back to 0000 instead of going on to 10–15. A counter moves on each rising edge of CLK while EN is high, or on each falling edge of EN while CLK is low. That second way is the trick for chaining: the units' Q4 (the "8") drops when going 9 → 0, so wired to the tens' EN it adds one ten. RESET (high) sends it back to 0.

Water analogy: a row of buckets that each hold 9 cups. Every tick pours one cup into the first bucket. When it gets its 10th cup, it tips over, empties itself, and pours one cup into the next bucket: the units carry over into the tens.

Untick "chain" and counter B never moves: the carry is just one wire. Its cousins: the CD4017 (one output per count, above), and the CD4510 (counts up or down, can be preset, not sold at Gotronic). Used in project 19: counter 00–99.

13CD4013 dual D flip-flop: a 1-bit memory with a camera shutter

What it does: two D flip-flops. Each one remembers one bit. At the exact moment its clock rises (low → high), it copies its D input to its Q output, and keeps it there until the next rising edge, whatever D does in between. Q̅ is always the opposite of Q. Two extra pins force it: S (set, Q = 1) and R (reset, Q = 0); keep them low for normal use.

The divide-by-2 trick: wire Q̅ back to D. Now D always asks for "the opposite of what Q is now", so every clock edge flips Q: 0, 1, 0, 1… Q changes once per clock pulse, so it makes one full cycle every two clock pulses: half the frequency. That's how project 26 turns the CD4060's 2 Hz into exactly 1 Hz. The CD4060 and CD4518 are built from exactly these flip-flops.

Water analogy: a camera pointed at a tap. D is the tap (open or closed); the clock is the shutter button. The photo (Q) shows the tap as it was at the click, and stays on the screen until the next click. In toggle mode it is a ballpoint pen: each click swaps in ↔ out.

Change D, then send clock pulses: Q only changes on the rising edge. Tick "toggle" to wire Q̅ → D and watch Q run at half the clock speed.

Other uses: a push-on / push-off switch (one button press = one clock; you need a debounced button, because a raw button bounces and gives several clocks), storing a sensor state at a precise moment, or a 2-bit counter with both halves chained.

14CD4060 oscillator + 14-stage divider: quartz time

What it does: two things in one chip. An oscillator (pins 9–11) that makes a fast clock with a quartz crystal, or with a resistor and a capacitor. And a chain of 14 flip-flops, each one halving the frequency of the one before (like the CD4013 in toggle mode). Ten of those stages come out on pins: Q4 to Q10 and Q12 to Q14.

How it works inside: the oscillator is an inverter (a NOT gate): its output is always the opposite of its input. Put a quartz from output to input and it keeps "correcting" itself back and forth, at the one frequency the quartz accepts. Every output Qn gives the oscillator frequency divided by 2ⁿ. With a 32.768 kHz watch quartz: Q4 = 2,048 Hz (a tone), Q10 = 32 Hz, Q14 = exactly 2 Hz. Add one more ÷2 (a CD4013) and you have 1 Hz: a clock's second.

Water analogy: a pendulum clock. The quartz is the pendulum, the inverter is the little kick that keeps it swinging, and the 14 stages are the gears that turn its fast ticks into seconds.

Fourteen stages, each half as fast as the one before. The first ones are far too fast for your eyes (they look half lit); watch the last ones blink. The grey stages exist inside but have no pin.

🔷 What is a quartz crystal, and why is it so precise?

A quartz is a thin slice of quartz crystal (the mineral, silicon dioxide) with a metal film on each side. Quartz is piezoelectric: squeeze it and it makes a small voltage; put a voltage on it and it bends a tiny bit. The same effect makes a piezo buzzer click (see Sound & motion).

The slice is cut so that it rings, mechanically, at one very exact frequency, like a tuning fork. A watch quartz is literally a tiny tuning fork inside the little metal cylinder. Electrically, it lets through almost nothing except that one frequency. So the inverter around it can only oscillate there: the quartz decides the rhythm, the chip just keeps it going.

  • Why 32,768 Hz? It's 2¹⁵: divide by 2 fifteen times and you get exactly 1 Hz. It's also slow enough for a tiny, very low-power watch circuit.
  • Precision: about ±20 ppm (parts per million): less than 2 seconds a day. A 555 with a resistor and a capacitor is off by 5–10 % and drifts with temperature and battery voltage: minutes a day.
  • The parts around it: the 10 MΩ biases the inverter to its middle point so it amplifies (it can't start otherwise). The two 22 pF capacitors are the "load" the quartz is tuned for. The 100 kΩ limits how hard the inverter shakes the tiny crystal: too much drive can damage a watch quartz.
  • Elsewhere: the Arduino's 16 MHz crystal (ATmega328P), every computer, phone, radio and watch. Faster crystals are thicker slices, not forks.

Push the quartz at different frequencies. Away from its own frequency, even by a few hertz, it barely moves. That razor-sharp response is why it's so precise.

Used in project 26: quartz stopwatch. Keep the quartz legs short and don't touch it while it runs: your finger adds capacitance and can stop it.

1574HC595 shift register: 8 outputs from 3 wires

What it does: a microcontroller often doesn't have enough pins. The 595 lets you control 8 outputs with just 3 wires. Chain several and you get 16, 24 or more, still with 3 wires.

How it works inside (3 steps):

  1. Put a bit (1 or 0) on the DATA wire.
  2. Pulse the CLOCK wire. Every bit inside shifts one place along, and the new bit enters at the start. Repeat 8 times.
  3. Pulse the LATCH wire. All 8 bits are copied to the outputs at the same moment, so the LEDs don't flicker while bits are moving.

Water analogy: a bucket brigade. Cups of water (1) or empty cups (0) are passed down a line of 8 people, one step per clock. When the line is full, everyone pours into their own tank at the same time (the latch).

Click the LEDs at the bottom to choose a pattern, then press Send.

16L293D H-bridge: a motor forwards and backwards

What it does: it drives two DC motors in both directions, from logic signals (buttons, an Arduino). Up to 600 mA per motor (1.2 A peaks), with a motor supply from 4.5 to 36 V on pin 8 and a separate logic supply on pin 16. The "D" means the flyback diodes are built in: no extra diodes for the motor's switch-off spikes.

How it works inside: the H-bridge. To reverse a DC motor, you reverse the current through it. An H-bridge is four switches arranged like the letter H, with the motor as the middle bar. Close top-left and bottom-right: current flows left → right. Close the other diagonal: right → left. The L293D has four "half-bridges" (1Y, 2Y, 3Y, 4Y): each output is switched either to + or to ground, copying its input. Two outputs make one H-bridge. Because each output is either + or −, never both, you can't make the deadly short circuit of closing both switches on one side.

Water analogy: a water wheel in a pipe between two tanks. Raise the left tank and lower the right: it turns one way. Swap them: it turns the other way. Same level on both sides: it stops. The L293D is the one lifting the tanks.

17TP4056 Li-ion charger: filling a battery safely

What it does: it charges a single Li-ion / Li-po cell (like an 18650) from 5 V USB, the safe way: first with a constant current (up to 1 A), then with a constant voltage of exactly 4.2 V while the current fades, then it stops. Two pins drive a red "charging" and a green "done" LED. It's the chip on the charger module of real-life build A, a tiny 8-pin SOP soldered on the board.

How it works inside: a power transistor between VCC and BAT acts as an adjustable valve. A control loop watches the battery: below 4.2 V it regulates the current (set by the PROG resistor: I = 1200 ÷ R); at 4.2 V it switches to holding the voltage. When the current falls to a tenth of the setting, the cell is full: CHRG turns off, STDBY turns on. A thermal loop lowers the current if the chip gets too hot.

Water analogy: filling a bottle from a tap: full flow at first, then you slow down as you approach the brim, and stop exactly at the line. Over-filling a Li-ion cell (above ~4.25 V) is dangerous, hence the precise chip.

The TP4056 does not protect against over-discharge or short circuits: that's the job of the separate protection chip (DW01A + dual MOSFET) on the BMS module, or on "protected" TP4056 boards (6 pins: IN+, IN−, B+, B−, OUT+, OUT−).

18ATmega328P: the brain of the Arduino

What it does: it's a complete little computer on one chip: a processor, 32 KB of memory for your program, 2 KB of working memory, and 23 input/output pins that your code can switch, read, time and measure. The Arduino Uno (and the ONE-C, with its cousin the ATmega328PB) is just this chip plus USB, a 16 MHz crystal and a 5 V regulator. It runs the dice (project 12) and the thermometer (project 20).

How it works inside: a CPU reads your program from Flash memory and executes ~16 million simple instructions per second, ticking with the crystal. It talks to the outside through ports (B, C, D): each pin is a tiny switch to 5 V or 0 V (digitalWrite), or an input (digitalRead). Some pins have extras: an ADC (analogRead, 6 pins A0–A5, 10 bits = 0–1023), timers for PWM (analogWrite on 6 pins), serial (TX/RX to the PC).

Water analogy: a control room with 23 valves and gauges, and an operator (the CPU) who follows a written procedure (your program) 16 million steps per second.

The demo runs a tiny program: value = analogRead(A0); analogWrite(9, value / 4); digitalWrite(13, millis() / 500 % 2);. Arduino pin names (D0–D13, A0–A5) are not the chip's pin numbers: D13 is chip pin 19, A0 is pin 23.

19Recap

  • A chip is a whole circuit (mostly transistors) in one package. Pin 1 is by the notch, numbered anticlockwise. Always connect power and add a 100 nF capacitor beside it.
  • NE555: fills and drains a capacitor between ⅓ and ⅔, which gives blinks, tones and delays.
  • 7805: an automatic valve that holds exactly 5 V, turning the extra into heat.
  • LM358: compares two voltages (yes/no) or amplifies precisely with feedback.
  • LM339: four comparators with open-collector outputs: each one pulls to ground when − is above +.
  • LM386: push-pull power to drive a speaker, with gain 20–200 (your Ruby amp).
  • CD4017: moves a single "on" along 10 outputs, one per clock pulse.
  • CD4511: turns a 4-bit number into the 7 segments of a digit.
  • CD4518: two decimal counters (0–9) that chain into 00–99.
  • CD4013: two 1-bit memories that copy D to Q on the clock edge; Q̅ → D makes a divide-by-2.
  • CD4060: a quartz oscillator plus 14 divide-by-2 stages: 32.768 kHz → 2 Hz.
  • 74HC595: shifts bits in one by one, then latches all 8 outputs at once.
  • LM3914: 10 comparators on a resistor ladder turn a voltage into a bar of LEDs.
  • L293D: two H-bridges: each motor end is switched to + or −, so the motor can turn both ways.
  • TP4056: charges a Li-ion cell with constant current, then exactly 4.2 V.
  • ATmega328P: a whole computer on a chip, the brain of the Arduino.
  • The LM35 temperature chip is on the Sensors page.
