#The short version

Buzzers, speakers and motors turn electricity into something you can hear or feel. A buzzer or a speaker pushes the air back and forth (sound). A motor turns a shaft (motion). Most of them work with a coil and a magnet: the same force that pulls a relay arm.

They are loads: they need far more current than a chip pin can give (a pin: ~20 mA; a speaker or a motor: hundreds of mA). So they are almost always driven through a transistor, an amplifier chip (LM386) or a motor driver (L293D). And because the coil inside is an inductor, it kicks back when switched off: hence the flyback diode.

#What is it for, and why use one?

🔔

Alarms and beeps

Why: an active buzzer beeps as soon as it gets DC: one transistor and you have a microwave "done" beep, a smoke alarm or a reversing warning.

🎵

Tones and melodies

Why: a passive buzzer or piezo disc plays whatever frequency you feed it: a 555 siren, Arduino tunes, a doorbell.

🔊

Music and voice

Why: only a speaker can follow a full audio signal, from deep bass to cymbals. It's the end of every amplifier, including your Ruby.

⚙️

Movement

Why: a DC motor spins fans, toy cars, pumps and drills. Reverse the current and it reverses; give it less power and it slows down.

#Buzzers: active or passive?

Two buzzers can look identical and behave completely differently:

  • Active buzzer (like the GT1143 module): it contains its own little oscillator. Give it steady DC (5 V) and it beeps on its own, always at the same pitch (~2.3 kHz). Easy, but you can't change the note.
  • Passive buzzer (like the PT2130PQ) and bare piezo discs: no oscillator inside. Steady DC only makes one click. You must feed it a square wave (a 555, Arduino tone()), and you choose the pitch.

Telling them apart: the active one is often taller with its underside sealed in black resin, and its + leg is longer or marked. A passive one often shows a green circuit board underneath. Or test it: 5 V for a second. A continuous beep = active, a click = passive.

Inside, two technologies: a piezo buzzer is a thin ceramic disc that bends when you put a voltage on it (the same piezoelectric effect as a quartz crystal). It draws very little current and can be driven straight from a pin. A magnetic buzzer is a small coil pulling a metal disc, like a tiny speaker: it needs more current, a transistor, and a flyback diode (it's a coil!).

Water analogy: the active buzzer is a toy whistle with its own pump: open the tap and it whistles. The passive buzzer is a bare whistle: it only sings if you blow into it at the right rhythm.

#The speaker: a coil that pushes air

A speaker is a coil glued to a paper or plastic cone, sitting in the gap of a strong permanent magnet. Current one way pushes the coil (and the cone) out; current the other way pulls it in. Feed it an audio signal (AC that wiggles hundreds or thousands of times per second) and the cone follows every wiggle, pushing the air into pressure waves: sound.

  • 8 Ω is the speaker's impedance: roughly its resistance to an audio signal. It's very low, so it needs a lot of current: that's the job of an amplifier like the LM386 (project 10) or your Ruby's.
  • Never put DC on it. 9 V across 8 Ω is over 1 A: the cone just sticks out, nothing sounds, and the thin coil overheats. That's why amps feed the speaker through a big capacitor (220 µF), which blocks the DC and lets the music through.
  • 0.5 W (the HP508) is the most power it can take without damage. A bigger cone moves more air: more volume and deeper bass.

Water analogy: a rubber membrane in a pipe, pushed back and forth by the water. It can't let water through, but each push makes a wave on the other side.

#The DC motor: magnets that chase each other

Inside a small DC motor (like the RE140 of projects 23–25) there are two curved permanent magnets on the case, and a spinning part, the rotor, made of coils. Powered coils become electromagnets that are pulled towards the opposite pole of the fixed magnets. Just as a coil arrives, two little carbon brushes rubbing on a split ring (the commutator) swap its current, so it's pushed onwards again instead of stopping. Result: continuous rotation.

  • Speed follows the voltage, roughly: 9 V → full speed, 4.5 V → about half.
  • Current follows the load. A spinning motor also works as a generator: it makes a voltage that pushes back against the battery (the back-EMF). Spinning freely, it pushes back almost as hard as the battery and draws little current. Hold the shaft and that back-push disappears: the current shoots up to the stall current, many times higher.
  • Reverse the wires, reverse the rotation. To do it electronically: an H-bridge (L293D, project 24).
  • It's a coil: switching it off makes a voltage spike. Put a 1N4007 across it (flyback diode), and a 100 nF across its tabs against the electrical noise of the brushes.
  • Slowing it down: not with a resistor (it wastes energy and kills the strength). Use PWM: full voltage switched on and off very fast (project 23), or a lower supply voltage (project 25).

Water analogy: a water wheel. More pressure (voltage) spins it faster. A free wheel lets little water through; block it with your hand and the full flow rushes through the pipe (the stall current).

#Recap

  • Active buzzer: has its own oscillator, beeps on DC, one fixed pitch.
  • Passive buzzer / piezo: needs a square wave, and you choose the pitch.
  • Speaker: a coil on a cone in a magnet. It follows AC audio, never DC, and needs an amplifier (8 Ω is a heavy load).
  • DC motor: speed follows voltage, current follows load. Reverse the current to reverse it, and use PWM to slow it down.
  • All the coil-based ones (magnetic buzzer, motor) need a flyback diode and a transistor or driver.
