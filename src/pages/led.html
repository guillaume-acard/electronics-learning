The LED

Light Emitting Diode: a one-way valve that glows

← All components

1The short version

An LED is a diode that gives off light when current flows through it forwards. It only works one way round, and its brightness depends on the current, not the voltage.

The golden rule: an LED always needs something to limit its current, usually a resistor. Connect it straight to a battery and it burns out within a second. (Everything about one-way valves is on the diode page.)

2What is it for, and why use one?

🔴

Indicator lights

"Power on", "charging", "recording". Why: tiny, cheap, uses very little power (a few mA) and lasts for years. Your Ruby amp has one!

💡

Lighting

Bulbs, torches, car lights. Why: roughly 5–10× more efficient than old filament bulbs, so less heat and more light.

📺

Screens

TVs, phones and big stadium displays are made of millions of tiny red, green and blue LEDs mixed together.

📡

Remote controls

An infrared LED (invisible to you) blinks a code to the TV. Look at it through a phone camera and you can see it flash!

🔌

Optocouplers

An LED shining on a light sensor inside one chip passes a signal across with no electrical connection. Used for safety in chargers.

3Water analogy, and why it needs a resistor

Picture the diode's one-way valve, with a little glowing paddle wheel behind it. The more water flows, the faster it spins and the brighter it glows. But the valve opens very suddenly: below ~2 V almost nothing flows, and just a bit above, a flood rushes through.

  • Directly on a voltage: a tiny change in voltage makes a huge change in current. It's impossible to control, and the LED burns.
  • Through a resistor: the resistor (a narrow pipe) limits the flood. Now the current rises gently and predictably with the voltage.

Try "Directly": move from 1.8 V to 2.1 V, a tiny move, and watch the current explode past 20 mA. Then try "Through a 150 Ω resistor" and sweep the whole range.

4Colours, forward voltage and the resistor calculator

Each colour needs a different push to light up (the forward voltage, Vf). Bluer light carries more energy, so it needs more voltage:

Infrared≈ 1.2 V
🔴 Red≈ 1.8–2.0 V
🟡 Yellow / 🟠 orange≈ 2.0–2.1 V
🟢 Green≈ 2.0–3.2 V (depends on type)
🔵 Blue / ⚪ white≈ 3.0–3.3 V

The formula: the resistor takes whatever voltage the LED doesn't. R = (Supply − Vf) ÷ Current

Standard resistors only come in certain values (1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 2.7, 3.3, 3.9, 4.7, 5.6, 6.8, 8.2 × 10ⁿ), so pick the next value up to stay safe. For indicator lights, 2–10 mA is plenty: modern LEDs are bright.

5Which leg is which?

Long leg = anode (+), which goes towards the positive supply. Short leg and flat edge = cathode (−), towards ground. Inside, the bigger "anvil" piece is usually the cathode. If it doesn't light, try turning it round: backwards, it simply blocks (it won't get damaged at low voltages).

6Simple example circuits

A. Several LEDs: one resistor each

It's tempting to share one resistor between LEDs in parallel. But no two LEDs are identical, and because the current rises so steeply, the one with the slightly lowest Vf hogs most of the current. It overheats and dies, then the next one takes over. Fix: give each LED its own resistor.

B. Dimming by blinking fast (PWM)

To dim an LED, chips don't lower the voltage. They switch it fully on and off very fast. Your eye averages the flashes: on 25% of the time looks like 25% brightness. This is called PWM (pulse width modulation). Why: it's efficient and precise, and it's how phones dim screens and Arduinos fade LEDs.

C. RGB LED: mixing colours

An RGB LED has a red, a green and a blue LED in one package. Dim each one with PWM and your eye mixes them into any colour. That's exactly what every pixel on a screen does.

8Recap

  • An LED is a diode that glows: one direction only, long leg to +.
  • Brightness follows the current, and the current rises extremely steeply with voltage.
  • So: always use a resistor. R = (supply − Vf) ÷ current.
  • Each colour has its own Vf (red ~2 V, blue/white ~3.2 V).
  • One resistor per LED. Dim with fast blinking (PWM). Mix red, green and blue to make any colour.
