The Transistor

A valve controlled by electricity: the building block of all modern electronics

← All components

1The short version

A transistor is a valve that a small current can open. A tiny current into one leg (the base) controls a much bigger current flowing between the other two legs (the collector → emitter).

That gives it two superpowers:

  • Switch: a weak signal, such as a chip's pin, turns a big load on and off (a motor, a lamp, a relay).
  • Amplifier: the big current follows the small one, but about 100× bigger. A faint guitar signal becomes a strong one.

This page uses the classic NPN bipolar transistor (BJT). Its cousin the MOSFET works the same way, but it's opened by a voltage on its gate instead of a current.

2What is it for, and why use one?

🔌

Switching big loads

Why: a microcontroller pin can give about 20 mA, but a motor needs 500 mA. A transistor lets the weak pin control the strong current safely.

🔊

Amplifying

Why: a guitar pickup or microphone gives a tiny signal. Transistors make it big enough to drive a speaker. Your Ruby amp's LM386 chip has a dozen inside!

🧠

Computing

Why: on/off switches can build logic (AND, OR, NOT). A phone's processor has billions of tiny transistors.

🌗

Reacting to sensors

Why: a sensor's weak signal (light, heat) can switch something on automatically, like a night light.

3The water analogy: a big valve opened by a small flow

A big pipe (collector → emitter) has a heavy gate in it. A small side pipe (the base) pushes a piston that lifts the gate. A trickle in the small pipe lets a river through the big one.

  • Off (cutoff): no base flow, gate closed, nothing flows.
  • Amplifying (active): the main flow = gain × base flow. With a gain of 100, 50 µA in gives 5 mA through. Double the base flow and you double the main flow.
  • Fully on (saturated): the gate is wide open. More base flow can't help, because the rest of the circuit (the load) now limits the flow. This is the "switch on" state.

One more detail: the base-emitter junction is a diode inside the transistor, so the base needs about 0.7 V before any base current flows (see the diode page). That's also why you always put a resistor in front of the base: to limit that current, just like with an LED.

4Simple example circuits

A. Switching a motor from a microcontroller

A microcontroller pin (Arduino, ESP32…) can give about 20 mA. A small motor needs ~500 mA. Connected directly, the pin can't deliver, the motor barely twitches, and the chip overheats. With a transistor, the pin only supplies a few mA into the base, and the transistor switches the 500 mA from the battery. The flyback diode across the motor protects the transistor (see the diode page).

The full circuit has two power sources: 5 V for the microcontroller and 9 V for the motor. The important detail is the common ground: both batteries' minus terminals, the chip's GND and the transistor's emitter all connect together. Without it, the base current from the pin has no way back to the chip, and the transistor never turns on. The big motor current loops through the 9 V battery, motor and transistor only. It never passes through the chip.

B. Amplifying a signal

A tiny wiggle on the base makes a big wiggle at the collector. The output is upside down (when the input goes up, the transistor opens more and pulls the output down), but it's the same shape, just bigger. Turn the gain up too far and the output hits the supply limits and gets clipped, which is another way to get distortion.

This is the classic common-emitter amplifier. Each part has a job:

  • R1 and R2 (bias): a voltage divider holding the base at about 1.5 V, so the transistor sits half-open. That way the signal can open it more and less. Without bias, the negative half of the wave would just switch it off.
  • C in and C out: coupling capacitors. They let the music in and out while blocking the DC bias voltages (just like example C on the capacitor page).
  • Rc (collector resistor): turns the transistor's changing current into a changing voltage. More current means a bigger voltage drop across Rc, so the collector goes down, which is why the output is upside down.
  • Re (emitter resistor): sets the gain: gain ≈ Rc ÷ Re. The slider changes Re: a smaller Re gives more gain.

🎸 In your Ruby amp, the first part (the J310 JFET) is a transistor used as a buffer: it doesn't add much gain, but it lets the guitar's weak, fragile signal drive the rest of the circuit without being loaded down. Then the LM386 chip, made of transistors inside, provides gain of 20 to 200×.

C. Automatic night light

An LDR (light-dependent resistor) and a resistor form a voltage divider on the base. In daylight the LDR's resistance is low and pulls the base below 0.7 V, so the transistor is off. When it gets dark, the LDR's resistance shoots up, the base voltage rises above 0.7 V, and the transistor turns the LED on.

D. Logic gates: how computers think

Use transistors as on/off switches and you can build decisions. Two in series (one after the other): both must be on for current to flow, which is AND. Two in parallel (side by side): either one is enough, which is OR. Combine millions of these and you get a computer.

5Key facts

NPN symbol: the arrow on the emitter points out (the direction current leaves). On PNP it points in.

LegsBase (control), Collector (in), Emitter (out). The order differs between models: always check the datasheet!
Gain (hFE, β)Typically 100–300: collector current ≈ gain × base current.
Base voltage≈ 0.7 V to turn on (there's a diode inside).
LimitsMax collector current (BC547: 100 mA, 2N2222: 600 mA), max voltage and max power (heat).

Common types

NPN BJT (BC547, 2N2222)The classic: switching small loads and simple amplifiers. Current-controlled.
PNP BJT (BC557, 2N2907)The mirror image: switches the + side. Its base is pulled low to turn it on.
N-channel MOSFET (IRLZ44N)Voltage-controlled, with almost no gate current. Great for big loads (motors, LED strips).
JFET (J310, MPF102)Very high input resistance, quiet. Used as a guitar input buffer, as in your Ruby amp.
Darlington (TIP120)Two transistors in one, so the gains multiply (~1000×). Easy to drive.
Inside chipsAn LM386 has about a dozen. A phone processor has billions, each a few nanometres wide.

6Recap

  • A transistor is a valve opened by a small current (or a voltage, for a MOSFET).
  • Three states: off, amplifying (main flow = gain × base flow) and fully on (limited by the load).
  • As a switch, a weak signal controls a big load. As an amplifier, a small wiggle becomes a big one.
  • The base needs ~0.7 V and always a base resistor.
  • Combined as switches, they make logic: the basis of every computer.
