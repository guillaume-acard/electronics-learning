The Resistor

The simplest component: it makes it harder for electricity to flow.

Unit: ohm (Ω)💧 A narrow pipeV = I × R

1The short version

A resistor resists the flow of electricity. It's a deliberately narrow pipe in the circuit. The narrower the pipe (the bigger the resistance), the less current flows for the same push.

Its strength is measured in ohms (Ω). A resistor doesn't store anything (that's the capacitor's job). It simply slows the flow down and turns the "lost" energy into a little heat.

2What is it for, and why use one?

A battery pushes as hard as it can. Many parts would take far too much current and burn, or would get the wrong voltage. Resistors are how we control the flow. They do six main jobs:

🛡️

1. Limit current

Why: an LED connected straight to a battery takes too much current and burns in a second. A resistor in series keeps the current safe.
Water: a narrow section so the flow can't rush.

➗

2. Divide a voltage

Why: two resistors in a row split a voltage into a smaller one, for example 9 V → 4.5 V. A volume knob is an adjustable divider.
Water: measuring the pressure halfway down a narrowing pipe.

📌

3. Set a default (pull-up)

Why: a chip's input left unconnected "floats" and reads random values. A resistor gently holds it at a known level until a button overrides it.
Water: a gentle spring holding a valve in place.

⏱️

4. Timing (with a capacitor)

Why: a resistor controls how fast a capacitor fills. Fill time = R × C, which is how blink rates and delays are set.
Water: a thin hose filling a tank slowly.

🔥

5. Make heat on purpose

Why: toasters, kettles, heaters and hair dryers are just big resistors. All the energy they slow down becomes heat.
Water: friction in a narrow pipe.

🌡️

6. Sense things

Why: some resistors change with light (LDR), temperature (thermistor) or pressure. The circuit measures the change to "see" or "feel".
Water: a pipe that narrows when it's cold.

3The water analogy: a narrow pipe

The pump (battery) creates pressure (voltage). The water flow is the current. The narrow section is the resistor.

  • Stronger pump → more flow.
  • Narrower pipe (more ohms) → less flow.
  • All the pressure is "used up" squeezing through the narrow part: high pressure before it, low pressure after it. That's called the voltage drop across the resistor.
  • The squeezing creates friction, which becomes heat.

Push harder and the flow speeds up. Narrow the pipe and it slows down. Both at once (high voltage and little resistance) gives lots of flow and lots of heat.

5Combining resistors: in a row or side by side

  • Series (one after the other): the water must squeeze through both narrow sections, so it's harder. The resistances add up: R = R1 + R2.
  • Parallel (side by side): the water has two paths to choose from, so it's easier. The total is smaller than either one: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2.

6The other "strength": the power rating (how much heat it can take)

Like the capacitor's voltage rating, a resistor has a limit, but this one is about heat. The heat produced is P = V × I, in watts. Most small resistors are rated ¼ W (0.25 W). Go above it and the resistor gets too hot: it discolours, smokes, and finally burns open, breaking the circuit.

7Simple example circuits

A. Protecting an LED (current limiting)

An LED needs about 2 V and at most 20 mA. A 9 V battery would push far too much current through it. The resistor takes the extra 7 V and limits the current to: I = (9 V − 2 V) ÷ R. Why: every LED in every device has a resistor like this.

No resistor gives an instant burn. 100 Ω is too much current, so it burns after a few seconds. 470 Ω is the right value. 10 kΩ is safe but dim.

B. Voltage divider and the volume knob (potentiometer)

Two resistors in a row split the voltage: Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2). A potentiometer is one long resistor with a sliding contact, so turning it moves the split point. Why: volume and tone knobs (including on a guitar), sensor readings, and setting reference levels.

C. Pull-up resistor (giving a chip a default value)

A button connects a chip's input to ground (0 V) when pressed. But when it's not pressed, the input is connected to nothing. It "floats" and picks up electrical noise, so the chip reads random HIGH/LOW values. A 10 kΩ pull-up to +5 V gently holds the input HIGH. Pressing the button wins easily, because the button is a direct path while the resistor only pulls gently. Why: it's on almost every button, switch and data line connected to a microcontroller.

D. Timing with a capacitor (the blinker)

A resistor controls how fast a capacitor fills (see the capacitor page). Here a 100 µF capacitor fills through the resistor. When it reaches ⅔ full, the LED flashes and it empties back to ⅓. The time scales with R × C, so a bigger resistor gives slower blinking. Why: blinkers, delays, oscillators and the classic 555 timer chip.

8Reading the colour code

Small resistors are too tiny for printed numbers, so they use coloured bands. The first two bands are digits, the third is the multiplier (how many zeros), and the fourth is the tolerance (how accurate it is).

Memory trick for the digit colours 0–9: Black Brown Red Orange Yellow Green Blue Violet Grey White. Surface-mount resistors use a number code instead: 103 = 10 followed by 3 zeros = 10 kΩ.

11Recap

  • A resistor is a narrow pipe: it limits how much current flows.
  • V = I × R: more voltage gives more current, and more resistance gives less current.
  • The voltage is "used up" across it (the voltage drop) and becomes heat.
  • Series: resistances add up (harder). Parallel: the total gets smaller (easier, more paths).
  • Respect the power rating (watts), or it overheats and burns.
  • Main jobs: protect LEDs, divide voltages (volume knobs), set defaults (pull-ups), and set timing with capacitors.

The Capacitor

A tiny, very fast rechargeable store of electric charge.

Unit: farad (F)💧 A rubber sheet in a pipeQ = C × V

Components

ΩResistorCCapacitorDDiode💡LEDTTransistorLInductorKRelay🔊Sound & motion

1The short version

A capacitor is two metal plates separated by an insulator. Electricity can't pass through the gap, but charge can pile up on the plates. It fills up quickly, holds the charge, and can release it all at once.

Think of it as a very small battery that charges and empties in a fraction of a second. A battery stores energy using chemistry. A capacitor stores it as charge sitting on the plates.

2What is it for, and why use one?

Batteries and power supplies are great at giving steady energy, but they are slow to react, and the electricity they deliver is never perfectly clean. Capacitors fix those problems. They do five main jobs:

⚡

1. Quick energy reserve

Why: some parts need a big burst instantly, such as a camera flash or a chip switching on. A battery can't deliver that fast enough, but a capacitor can empty in a split second.
Water: a bucket ready to dump, instead of waiting for a slow tap.

〰️

2. Smoothing

Why: power from a wall adapter comes in bumps. The capacitor fills during the peaks and gives back during the dips, so the voltage stays steady and devices don't glitch.
Water: a water tower that keeps pressure even when the pump pulses.

🚧

3. Blocking DC, passing signals

Why: in audio, you want the music to go from one stage to the next, but not the steady DC voltage, which would cause distortion or damage. A capacitor lets only the changing part through.
Water: the rubber sheet (see section 5).

🔇

4. Filtering noise and tone

Why: capacitors let fast wiggles through more easily than slow ones. Used cleverly, they dump hiss and buzz to ground, or cut treble (your guitar's tone knob).
Water: a side drain that only swallows quick ripples.

⏱️

5. Timing

Why: filling through a resistor always takes the same time, so circuits use it as a clock: blinking LEDs, delays, and the "hold" before lights turn off.
Water: an hourglass that always takes the same time to fill.

In one sentence: capacitors are used wherever a circuit needs to store a little energy for a moment, steady a voltage, separate signals from DC, or measure time. That's why almost every electronic device contains dozens of them.

3The water analogy: a rubber sheet in a pipe

Picture a water pipe with a stretchy rubber sheet sealed across the middle. Water cannot pass through the sheet.

  • Turn on the pump (the battery). Water rushes in on one side and stretches the sheet. The same amount of water is pushed out of the other side, so water flows around the whole loop.
  • As the sheet stretches, it pushes back harder and harder. The flow slows down, and when the sheet's push equals the pump's push, the flow stops. The capacitor is now full.
  • Remove the pump and the stretched sheet springs back, pushing the water the other way. That's discharging.

5Its superpower: blocks steady flow, lets wiggles through

📖 First: what are DC and AC?

DC: Direct CurrentFlows in one direction and stays steady. Like a river. Examples: batteries, USB (5 V), a phone charger's output.
AC: Alternating CurrentKeeps changing direction, back and forth. Like waves sloshing in a bathtub. Examples: the wall socket (230 V, 50 times a second in France), a guitar or music signal.

People also say "DC" for the steady part of a signal, and "AC" for the part that wiggles. A signal can be both at once: a wiggle riding on a steady level, as in example C below.

Because of the rubber sheet, a steady push (DC, like a battery) only moves water briefly, until the sheet is stretched. After that, nothing flows. A back-and-forth push (AC, like an audio signal) makes the sheet flex one way and then the other, forever. Water keeps sloshing, so the waterwheel keeps moving.

This is why guitar amps and audio gear put capacitors between stages. The music (a fast wiggle) passes through, while unwanted steady DC voltage is blocked. This job is called a coupling capacitor.

7Different sizes ("strengths") and what each is used for

Capacitors come in a huge range of sizes, from tiny to enormous. The "strength" of a capacitor means two different things:

  • Capacitance (size), in farads: how much charge it holds. In water terms, how big and stretchy the rubber sheet is.
  • Voltage rating, in volts: how much pressure it can take before it fails. In water terms, how thick and tough the sheet is.

Voltage rating: how tough the rubber sheet is

Every capacitor has a maximum voltage printed on it, such as 16V, 25V or 400V. Exceed it and the insulator breaks down, like a rubber sheet bursting under too much pressure. The part fails, and electrolytics can bulge or pop.

  • Rule of thumb: choose a rating around 1.5–2× the voltage in your circuit. For a 9 V circuit, use 16 V or 25 V caps.
  • A higher rating never hurts the circuit. It just means a physically bigger and pricier part. A thicker sheet needs more room.
  • That's why a 1000 µF 16 V capacitor is fingertip-sized, but a 1000 µF 400 V one is the size of a drinks can.

8Simple example circuits

Here are four small circuits you'll meet again and again. In each one you can swap the capacitor size and see what changes.

A. LED that fades out slowly (energy reserve)

A battery, a push button, and an LED with its resistor. A capacitor sits beside the LED. Pressing the button lights the LED and fills the capacitor. When you let go, the capacitor keeps feeding the LED for a while, so it fades instead of snapping off. Why: this is the "dim slowly" effect in car interior lights and the glow that lingers on a charger after you unplug it.

Bigger capacitor = bigger reserve = longer fade. 10 µF is gone almost instantly, while 1000 µF glows for seconds.

B. Power supply smoothing (the water tower)

Electricity from the wall is AC: it swings back and forth 50 times a second. A diode, a one-way valve, lets only the forward pushes through, which leaves a stream of bumps. A big capacitor fills at each bump and tops up the gaps in between, so the output is almost flat. Why: every phone charger, guitar amp and computer needs steady DC, and this is how they get it.

Slowed down so you can see it. With no capacitor the lamp flickers. Bigger capacitors give smaller dips ("ripple"), which is why power supplies use the bathtub-sized ones.

C. Coupling capacitor in audio (pass the music, block the DC)

Inside an amp, the guitar signal often rides on top of a steady DC voltage, which the circuit needs to work. Sending that DC into the next stage would upset it. A capacitor in series passes the wiggles (the music) and blocks the DC. The output is the same sound, now centred on zero. Why: it's used between almost every stage of an amplifier, and on the input of a guitar amp or pedal.

Size matters here too. A capacitor that is too small only lets fast wiggles (treble) through, so the bass disappears and the sound gets thin. That's also how a tone control works on purpose.

🔍 Why does the output end up centred on 0?

The signal is made of two parts: a steady 4.5 V (DC) plus a wiggle of ±0.5 V (the music), so it moves between 4 V and 5 V.

  1. The steady 4.5 V stretches the rubber sheet once and it stays stretched. The capacitor is now charged to 4.5 V. A steady push causes no more flow, so the sheet absorbs it.
  2. The wiggle makes the sheet flex a bit more, then a bit less. Each flex pushes water back and forth on the other side, so the wiggle gets through.
  3. The capacitor keeps holding 4.5 V, so the output is always input − 4.5 V:

If the output sat above 0 on average, current would flow through that resistor and charge the capacitor a bit more, pulling the output down. If it sat below 0, the opposite would happen. It only stops adjusting when the output averages exactly 0.

🚤 Boat analogy: you hold a rope tied to a boat on a lake. You feel the waves tugging back and forth, but never the height of the lake. The capacitor is the rope: it passes the movement on and ignores the level.

🤔 But I thought a full capacitor blocks current?

"Full" doesn't mean closed for good. It means in balance: the capacitor pushes back exactly as hard as the input pushes, so nothing flows.

  • Steady push (DC): the push never changes, so once balanced it stays balanced, and the current stops for good. That's how DC gets blocked.
  • Changing push (music): the balance keeps breaking. When the input rises above what the capacitor holds, a little charge flows in. When it drops below, a little flows back out. Current keeps sloshing back and forth.
  • No charge ever crosses the gap. Charge arriving on one plate pushes the same amount off the other plate. From outside it looks like current passed through, just as pushing the rubber sheet moves the water on the other side.

The wobble doesn't wait for the capacitor to fill. The capacitor does two jobs at the same time: it slowly absorbs the steady part and immediately passes the changes. (Don't confuse the capacitor's voltage, which is what it currently holds, with its voltage rating, which is its burst limit.)

Watch the two bars. When they're equal, nothing moves. With the steady push they become equal and stay that way. With music they never stay equal for long, so the charges keep moving on both sides of the gap.

📏 Why does the size (pF, nF, 100 nF) change the sound?

Rule: a capacitor blocks any change slow enough for it to catch up with. The capacitor always tries to match the input. When it matches, nothing gets through. So the question is: can it catch up before the wiggle turns around?

  • Small capacitor (a thimble): fills almost instantly. It keeps up with slow wiggles (bass) and cancels them. Only fast wiggles (treble) escape. Result: a thin, tinny sound.
  • Big capacitor (a bathtub): fills slowly and can't keep up with bass or treble. Everything passes. Result: a full sound.
  • DC is the slowest change there is, a push that never changes. Every capacitor eventually catches up with it, so DC is always blocked.

If they're slow (big capacitor), every push gets through. A guitar's tone knob uses this on purpose to separate bass from treble.

D. Decoupling capacitor next to a chip (a local reserve)

Chips such as microcontrollers and op-amps draw power in sudden little gulps. The power has to travel down a long wire, so it can't arrive fast enough, and the voltage at the chip briefly sags. If it sags too far, the chip glitches or resets. A small 100 nF capacitor placed right beside the chip supplies each gulp instantly. Why: it's a cheap fix for random crashes, so every chip's datasheet asks for one.

🤔 Why does a long wire make the voltage sag?

It's not mainly because the wire is "far". It's because the current in a wire has inertia, like the water in a long pipe.

  • Small reason: resistance. Every wire loses a little voltage (current × resistance). On a circuit board that's only a few millivolts, so it's not the real problem.
  • Big reason: inertia (called inductance). Picture a long pipe full of still water with a tap at the end. When the tap suddenly opens, the whole column of water has to speed up first. During that moment the pressure at the tap drops: it wants water now, but the pipe can't deliver it yet. It's like your shower going weak when someone opens another tap. A wire resists sudden changes in current in the same way, and the longer the wire, the more "water" there is to get moving.

Why chips care: chips switch in a few nanoseconds (billionths of a second), and at that speed even a few centimetres of wire counts as long. Rough numbers: a 10 cm wire has about 100 nH of inductance. If the chip suddenly wants 0.1 A more within 10 ns, the sag ≈ 100 nH × (0.1 A ÷ 10 ns) ≈ 1 V. On a 3.3 V or 5 V chip that's huge, enough to cause a glitch or reset. The power supply also needs time to react, so it can't fix the dip fast enough either.

The fix: a small tank right next to the tap. When the tap opens suddenly, the tank supplies the water instantly while the long pipe catches up, then the pipe refills it calmly. That's why the 100 nF capacitor must sit right beside the chip's power pin. If it's far away, there's still a stretch of pipe between the tank and the tap, and the problem is only partly fixed.

Watch the dashes in the long pipe. They speed up slowly after each gulp, which is the inertia. Compare the dip on the pressure gauge and the graph with no tank, with a tank far away, and with a tank right beside the chip.

1The short version

A diode lets current flow in one direction only. Forward it conducts, backwards it blocks. It's a one-way valve (a check valve) in the pipe.

There's one catch: the valve has a small spring, so it needs a little push to open. For a normal silicon diode that's about 0.7 V, the forward voltage. Below that, nothing flows even in the right direction.

The two legs are called the anode (+) and the cathode (−). Current flows from anode to cathode, in the direction the arrow in the symbol points. On the real part, the stripe marks the cathode.

2What is it for, and why use one?

🔄

1. AC → DC (rectifier)

Why: the wall gives AC (back and forth), but electronics need DC. Diodes let only one direction through. Every charger has some.
Water: check valves that turn sloshing into a one-way stream.

🔋

2. Reverse battery protection

Why: if someone puts the battery in backwards, the diode blocks it and the circuit survives.
Water: a valve that refuses backward flow.

⚡

3. Spike protection (flyback)

Why: motors and relays create a huge voltage spike when switched off. A diode gives that energy a safe path.
Water: a relief path for a water hammer.

🎸

4. Guitar distortion

Why: two diodes "clip" the tops off the signal, which is the classic overdrive and fuzz sound in pedals.
Water: relief valves that won't let the pressure go above a limit.

💡

5. Light (LED)

Why: some diodes glow when current flows. That's the LED, which has its own page.
Water: a one-way valve that lights up.

📏

6. Voltage reference (Zener)

Why: a Zener diode is made to conduct backwards at an exact voltage, such as 5.1 V. It's used to hold a steady reference voltage.
Water: a valve that opens backwards at a set pressure.

3The water analogy: a check valve

  • Forward push above ~0.7 V: the flap swings open and water flows.
  • Forward push below 0.7 V: the spring keeps the flap shut. Nothing flows yet.
  • Backward push: the flap is pressed against its seat, so it's sealed and nothing flows.

Right: the diode's "personality" graph. Nothing happens until about 0.6 V, then the current shoots up very steeply. That steepness is why diodes and LEDs need a resistor to limit the current.

4Simple example circuits

A. Reverse battery protection

A diode in series with the battery. Battery the right way: current flows (losing 0.7 V). Battery backwards: the diode blocks and the circuit is safe. Why: toys, remote controls and pedals all have this, because people do put batteries in backwards.

B. Turning AC into DC (rectifier)

With one diode (half-wave), only the forward half of each wave gets through, so half the energy is thrown away. With four diodes in a "bridge" (full-wave), the backward halves are flipped forward too, so nothing is wasted. Add a big capacitor afterwards to smooth the bumps (see the capacitor page, example B).

C. Flyback diode on a motor or relay

A motor or relay coil is like a heavy water wheel: once it's spinning, the water keeps pushing. Close the valve suddenly and you get a water hammer, a huge pressure spike. Electrically, switching off a coil creates a spike of hundreds of volts that can destroy the transistor or switch. A diode across the coil (pointing "backwards") gives the current a loop to fade out safely. Why: every relay and motor driver needs one.

D. Guitar distortion (clipping diodes)

Two diodes, pointing opposite ways, sit from the signal to ground. Small signals below 0.7 V pass untouched. Bigger signals get their tops sliced off at ±0.7 V, because the diodes open and drain the excess. The wave becomes squarer, which adds the harmonics we hear as overdrive and fuzz.

1The short version

3Water analogy, and why it needs a resistor

Picture the diode's one-way valve, with a little glowing paddle wheel behind it. The more water flows, the faster it spins and the brighter it glows. But the valve opens very suddenly: below ~2 V almost nothing flows, and just a bit above, a flood rushes through.

  • Directly on a voltage: a tiny change in voltage makes a huge change in current. It's impossible to control, and the LED burns.
  • Through a resistor: the resistor (a narrow pipe) limits the flood. Now the current rises gently and predictably with the voltage.

Try "Directly": move from 1.8 V to 2.1 V, a tiny move, and watch the current explode past 20 mA. Then try "Through a 150 Ω resistor" and sweep the whole range.

5Which leg is which?

Long leg = anode (+), which goes towards the positive supply. Short leg and flat edge = cathode (−), towards ground. Inside, the bigger "anvil" piece is usually the cathode. If it doesn't light, try turning it round: backwards, it simply blocks (it won't get damaged at low voltages).

This is called PWM (pulse width modulation). Why: it's efficient and precise, and it's how phones dim screens and Arduinos fade LEDs.

C. RGB LED: mixing colours

An RGB LED has a red, a green and a blue LED in one package. Dim each one with PWM and your eye mixes them into any colour. That's exactly what every pixel on a screen does.

The Transistor

A valve controlled by electricity: the building block of all modern electronics.

Gain ≈ 100×💧 A big valve, small leverSwitch or amplifier

Components

ΩResistorCCapacitorDDiode💡LEDTTransistorLInductorKRelay🔊Sound & motion

1The short version

A transistor is a valve that a small current can open. A tiny current into one leg (the base) controls a much bigger current flowing between the other two legs (the collector → emitter).

That gives it two superpowers:

  • Switch: a weak signal, such as a chip's pin, turns a big load on and off (a motor, a lamp, a relay).
  • Amplifier: the big current follows the small one, but about 100× bigger. A faint guitar signal becomes a strong one.

This page uses the classic NPN bipolar transistor (BJT). Its cousin the MOSFET works the same way, but it's opened by a voltage on its gate instead of a current.

2What is it for, and why use one?

🔌

Switching big loads

Why: a microcontroller pin can give about 20 mA, but a motor needs 500 mA. A transistor lets the weak pin control the strong current safely.

🔊

Amplifying

Why: a guitar pickup or microphone gives a tiny signal. Transistors make it big enough to drive a speaker. Your Ruby amp's LM386 chip has a dozen inside!

🧠

Computing

Why: on/off switches can build logic (AND, OR, NOT). A phone's processor has billions of tiny transistors.

🌗

Reacting to sensors

Why: a sensor's weak signal (light, heat) can switch something on automatically, like a night light.

3The water analogy: a big valve opened by a small flow

A big pipe (collector → emitter) has a heavy gate in it. A small side pipe (the base) pushes a piston that lifts the gate. A trickle in the small pipe lets a river through the big one.

  • Off (cutoff): no base flow, gate closed, nothing flows.
  • Amplifying (active): the main flow = gain × base flow. With a gain of 100, 50 µA in gives 5 mA through. Double the base flow and you double the main flow.
  • Fully on (saturated): the gate is wide open. More base flow can't help, because the rest of the circuit (the load) now limits the flow. This is the "switch on" state.

One more detail: the base-emitter junction is a diode inside the transistor, so the base needs about 0.7 V before any base current flows (see the diode page). That's also why you always put a resistor in front of the base: to limit that current, just like with an LED.

4Simple example circuits

A. Switching a motor from a microcontroller

A microcontroller pin (Arduino, ESP32…) can give about 20 mA. A small motor needs ~500 mA. Connected directly, the pin can't deliver, the motor barely twitches, and the chip overheats. With a transistor, the pin only supplies a few mA into the base, and the transistor switches the 500 mA from the battery. The flyback diode across the motor protects the transistor (see the diode page).

The full circuit has two power sources: 5 V for the microcontroller and 9 V for the motor. The important detail is the common ground: both batteries' minus terminals, the chip's GND and the transistor's emitter all connect together. Without it, the base current from the pin has no way back to the chip, and the transistor never turns on. The big motor current loops through the 9 V battery, motor and transistor only. It never passes through the chip.

B. Amplifying a signal

A tiny wiggle on the base makes a big wiggle at the collector. The output is upside down (when the input goes up, the transistor opens more and pulls the output down), but it's the same shape, just bigger. Turn the gain up too far and the output hits the supply limits and gets clipped, which is another way to get distortion.

This is the classic common-emitter amplifier. Each part has a job:

  • R1 and R2 (bias): a voltage divider holding the base at about 1.5 V, so the transistor sits half-open. That way the signal can open it more and less. Without bias, the negative half of the wave would just switch it off.
  • C in and C out: coupling capacitors. They let the music in and out while blocking the DC bias voltages (just like example C on the capacitor page).
  • Rc (collector resistor): turns the transistor's changing current into a changing voltage. More current means a bigger voltage drop across Rc, so the collector goes down, which is why the output is upside down.
  • Re (emitter resistor): sets the gain: gain ≈ Rc ÷ Re. The slider changes Re: a smaller Re gives more gain.

🎸 In your Ruby amp, the first part (the J310 JFET) is a transistor used as a buffer: it doesn't add much gain, but it lets the guitar's weak, fragile signal drive the rest of the circuit without being loaded down. Then the LM386 chip, made of transistors inside, provides gain of 20 to 200×.

C. Automatic night light

An LDR (light-dependent resistor) and a resistor form a voltage divider on the base. In daylight the LDR's resistance is low and pulls the base below 0.7 V, so the transistor is off. When it gets dark, the LDR's resistance shoots up, the base voltage rises above 0.7 V, and the transistor turns the LED on.

D. Logic gates: how computers think

Use transistors as on/off switches and you can build decisions. Two in series (one after the other): both must be on for current to flow, which is AND. Two in parallel (side by side): either one is enough, which is OR. Combine millions of these and you get a computer.

The Inductor

A coil of wire that stores energy in a magnetic field and hates sudden changes in current.

Unit: henry (H)💧 A heavy water wheelMirror of the capacitor

Components

ΩResistorCCapacitorDDiode💡LEDTTransistorLInductorKRelay🔊Sound & motion

1The short version

An inductor is a coil of wire. When current flows through it, it builds a magnetic field and stores energy there. Its personality: it hates sudden changes in current. It slows the current down when you switch on, and it fights to keep it going when you switch off.

It is the mirror image of the capacitor: a capacitor resists sudden changes in voltage, while an inductor resists sudden changes in current.

2What is it for, and why use one?

🔋

Boost and buck converters

Why: by charging a coil and releasing it quickly, you can turn 3.7 V into 5 V (power banks) or 12 V into 3.3 V very efficiently.
Water: a flywheel that is spun up, then used to pump water higher.

🔇

Filters and chokes

Why: fast wiggles (noise) can't get through easily, but steady DC can. That's the opposite of a capacitor. It's used to clean up power lines and in speaker crossovers (bass to the woofer).
Water: a heavy wheel that ignores quick jolts.

🔁

Transformers

Why: two coils share one magnetic core, so AC in one coil makes AC in the other, at a higher or lower voltage. Old chargers, guitar amps and the power grid all use them.

🧲

Electromagnets

Why: a coil makes a magnet when current flows. That's what inside relays, motors, speakers, solenoid valves and door locks.

📡

Radio and wireless charging

Why: a coil plus a capacitor resonates at one frequency, which is how radios tune in. Two coils facing each other transfer power through the air (phone wireless charging).

3The water analogy: a heavy water wheel in the pipe

Picture a heavy paddle wheel (a flywheel) inside the pipe. Water can only flow if the wheel turns.

  • Switch on: the wheel is heavy, so the water can't rush instantly. The flow builds up gradually.
  • Steady flow: once spinning, the wheel turns freely. For steady DC an inductor is just a wire.
  • Switch off suddenly: the wheel keeps spinning and slams water against the closed valve. That's a water hammer, a huge pressure spike. Electrically: hundreds of volts, and sparks.

Turn it ON and watch the current climb slowly. Turn it OFF without the diode: spike and sparks. Add the flyback diode, and the current circles through the diode and fades gently, just like on the diode page.

5Simple example circuits

A. Boost converter: 1.5 V lights a 3 V LED

A single AA battery (1.5 V) can't light a white LED (needs ~3 V). But a coil can: a transistor shorts the coil to ground for a moment (the current builds up), then lets go. The coil's "water hammer" adds its push on top of the battery, through the diode into the LED. Repeat thousands of times per second and the LED glows. This is the famous "Joule thief", and the same idea is inside every power bank.

B. Filtering noise (LC filter)

An inductor in series and a capacitor to ground: the inductor blocks fast wiggles, and the capacitor drains whatever gets through. Steady DC passes. Why: it cleans a noisy power supply before it reaches an audio amp or a sensor.

C. Transformer: changing AC voltage

Two coils on one iron core. AC in the first coil makes a changing magnetic field, which pushes AC into the second coil. Voltage ratio = turns ratio: 10× fewer turns on the output gives 10× less voltage. With DC the field doesn't change, so nothing comes out.

The Relay

A switch flipped by an electromagnet: a small current controls a separate big one.

COM · NO · NC💧 A lever on a separate pipeIsolated switching

Components

ΩResistorCCapacitorDDiode💡LEDTTransistorLInductorKRelay🔊Sound & motion

1The short version

A relay is a switch flipped by an electromagnet. A small current through its coil makes a magnet that pulls a metal arm, which closes (or opens) a completely separate circuit. You hear it go click.

The two sides are not electrically connected: a 5 V microcontroller can safely switch a 12 V motor or even a 230 V lamp. It combines the inductor (the coil) with a mechanical switch.

2What is it for, and why use one?

🏠

Switching mains appliances

Why: an Arduino can't touch 230 V, but a relay can switch a lamp, heater or pump while keeping the low-voltage side fully isolated.

🚗

Cars

Why: headlights, horn and starter need big currents. A thin wire from the dashboard switch drives a relay near the battery, which carries the heavy current.

🔀

Changeover

Why: one relay can switch between two circuits (NC ↔ NO), for example mains to backup battery, or speaker A to speaker B.

🧠

Simple memory and logic

Why: a relay can hold itself on (latching), as in machine start/stop buttons. The first computers were built from relays!

3How it works (and the water analogy)

  • COM (common) is the moving arm. NC (normally closed) is touched by the arm at rest. NO (normally open) is touched only when the coil is powered.
  • The coil needs a minimum voltage to pull in (e.g. ~3.75 V for a 5 V relay). Once pulled in, it holds until the voltage drops much lower (drop-out, ~1 V). This gap makes it snap cleanly instead of chattering.
  • Water analogy: a small pipe drives a little turbine that pulls a lever. The lever opens a valve on a completely separate big pipe. The two waters never mix.

Slide up slowly: nothing until about 3.75 V, then click. Now slide back down: it stays on until about 1 V. The NC lamp (red) and NO lamp (green) swap.

4Simple example circuits

A. A microcontroller switching a lamp through a relay

The relay coil needs ~70 mA, too much for a pin (20 mA). So we use a transistor to drive the coil (see the transistor page), plus a flyback diode across the coil because it's an inductor and kicks back when switched off. The lamp circuit is completely separate.

Ready-made "relay modules" for Arduino contain exactly this: the transistor, the diode and an LED, all on one small board.

B. Self-latching relay (start / stop)

Press START: the coil is powered, and the relay's own NO contact closes a path that keeps feeding its coil. Release START: it stays on. Press STOP (a normally-closed button) to break the loop and it drops out. That's a 1-bit memory, used on machine tools and garage doors.

Buzzers, Speakers & Motors

Parts that turn electricity into something you hear or feel: a coil (or a crystal) pushing on air, or on a shaft.

Components

ΩResistorCCapacitorDDiode💡LEDTTransistorLInductorKRelay🔊Sound & motion

1The short version

Buzzers, speakers and motors turn electricity into something you can hear or feel. A buzzer or a speaker pushes the air back and forth (sound). A motor turns a shaft (motion). Most of them work with a coil and a magnet: the same force that pulls a relay arm.

They are loads: they need far more current than a chip pin can give (a pin: ~20 mA; a speaker or a motor: hundreds of mA). So they are almost always driven through a transistor, an amplifier chip (LM386) or a motor driver (L293D). And because the coil inside is an inductor, it kicks back when switched off: hence the flyback diode.

2What is it for, and why use one?

🔔

Alarms and beeps

Why: an active buzzer beeps as soon as it gets DC: one transistor and you have a microwave "done" beep, a smoke alarm or a reversing warning.

🎵

Tones and melodies

Why: a passive buzzer or piezo disc plays whatever frequency you feed it: a 555 siren, Arduino tunes, a doorbell.

🔊

Music and voice

Why: only a speaker can follow a full audio signal, from deep bass to cymbals. It's the end of every amplifier, including your Ruby.

⚙️

Movement

Why: a DC motor spins fans, toy cars, pumps and drills. Reverse the current and it reverses; give it less power and it slows down.

3Buzzers: active or passive?

Two buzzers can look identical and behave completely differently:

  • Active buzzer (like the GT1143 module): it contains its own little oscillator. Give it steady DC (5 V) and it beeps on its own, always at the same pitch (~2.3 kHz). Easy, but you can't change the note.
  • Passive buzzer (like the PT2130PQ) and bare piezo discs: no oscillator inside. Steady DC only makes one click. You must feed it a square wave (a 555, Arduino tone()), and you choose the pitch.

Telling them apart: the active one is often taller with its underside sealed in black resin, and its + leg is longer or marked. A passive one often shows a green circuit board underneath. Or test it: 5 V for a second. A continuous beep = active, a click = passive.

Inside, two technologies: a piezo buzzer is a thin ceramic disc that bends when you put a voltage on it (the same piezoelectric effect as a quartz crystal). It draws very little current and can be driven straight from a pin. A magnetic buzzer is a small coil pulling a metal disc, like a tiny speaker: it needs more current, a transistor, and a flyback diode (it's a coil!).

Water analogy: the active buzzer is a toy whistle with its own pump: open the tap and it whistles. The passive buzzer is a bare whistle: it only sings if you blow into it at the right rhythm.

"Listen" plays the sound for one second through your computer (quietly). Try the passive buzzer at 300 Hz, then 3,000 Hz, and the active one on DC. Used in project 8, project 11 and the IR alarm.

4The speaker: a coil that pushes air

A speaker is a coil glued to a paper or plastic cone, sitting in the gap of a strong permanent magnet. Current one way pushes the coil (and the cone) out; current the other way pulls it in. Feed it an audio signal (AC that wiggles hundreds or thousands of times per second) and the cone follows every wiggle, pushing the air into pressure waves: sound.

  • 8 Ω is the speaker's impedance: roughly its resistance to an audio signal. It's very low, so it needs a lot of current: that's the job of an amplifier like the LM386 (project 10) or your Ruby's.
  • Never put DC on it. 9 V across 8 Ω is over 1 A: the cone just sticks out, nothing sounds, and the thin coil overheats. That's why amps feed the speaker through a big capacitor (220 µF), which blocks the DC and lets the music through.
  • 0.5 W (the HP508) is the most power it can take without damage. A bigger cone moves more air: more volume and deeper bass.

Water analogy: a rubber membrane in a pipe, pushed back and forth by the water. It can't let water through, but each push makes a wave on the other side.

The animation is slowed down enormously: real sound is 20 to 20,000 pushes per second. Used in project 10: mini amp and project 17: police siren.

5The DC motor: magnets that chase each other

Inside a small DC motor (like the RE140 of projects 23–25) there are two curved permanent magnets on the case, and a spinning part, the rotor, made of coils. Powered coils become electromagnets that are pulled towards the opposite pole of the fixed magnets. Just as a coil arrives, two little carbon brushes rubbing on a split ring (the commutator) swap its current, so it's pushed onwards again instead of stopping. Result: continuous rotation.

  • Speed follows the voltage, roughly: 9 V → full speed, 4.5 V → about half.
  • Current follows the load. A spinning motor also works as a generator: it makes a voltage that pushes back against the battery (the back-EMF). Spinning freely, it pushes back almost as hard as the battery and draws little current. Hold the shaft and that back-push disappears: the current shoots up to the stall current, many times higher.
  • Reverse the wires, reverse the rotation. To do it electronically: an H-bridge (L293D, project 24).
  • It's a coil: switching it off makes a voltage spike. Put a 1N4007 across it (flyback diode), and a 100 nF across its tabs against the electrical noise of the brushes.
  • Slowing it down: not with a resistor (it wastes energy and kills the strength). Use PWM: full voltage switched on and off very fast (project 23), or a lower supply voltage (project 25).

Water analogy: a water wheel. More pressure (voltage) spins it faster. A free wheel lets little water through; block it with your hand and the full flow rushes through the pipe (the stall current).

Push the load all the way: the motor stalls and the current jumps to its maximum. A stalled motor heats up fast, and that's the moment the battery, transistor or driver chip suffers most. The numbers are typical for a small RE140-type motor, not exact.

3Ohm's law: V = R × I

The one to know by heart: voltage = resistance × current. Rearranged: I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I.

The triangle trick: write V on top, R and I below. Cover the one you want: what's left tells you how. Cover V → R × I. Cover I → V ÷ R. Cover R → V ÷ I.

Water analogy: more pressure (V) pushes more flow (I); a narrower pipe (R) lets less through for the same pressure.

Solve for:

Examples from the projects

  • Base resistor (project 12): (5 − 0.7) V ÷ 1 kΩ = 4.3 mA into the transistor.
  • Pull-down (projects 13–15): 9 V ÷ 10 kΩ = 0.9 mA when the button is pressed, harmless.
  • Skin (project 5): 9 V ÷ 500 kΩ = 18 µA: too little for an LED, hence the Darlington pair.

4Power: P = V × I

Power = voltage × current. Combined with Ohm's law it has two other forms: P = I² × R and P = V² ÷ R.

Why it matters: power in a resistor, a regulator or a transistor turns into heat. Every part has a limit: ¼ W for the small resistors, ~0.5 W for a BC547, ~1 W for a 7805 without heatsink.

Water analogy: a water wheel turns harder with more pressure and more flow: power is the product of both.

Examples from the projects

  • LED + 470 Ω on 9 V: 7 V × 15 mA = 0.1 W in the resistor: fine for ¼ W.
  • Siren speaker (project 17): 220 Ω on a 9 V square wave: 9² ÷ 228 × ½ ≈ 0.18 W: that's why it's 220 Ω and not 100 Ω.
  • 7805 with 12 V in, 5 V out, 300 mA: (12 − 5) × 0.3 = 2.1 W of heat: it needs a heatsink.
  • LM3914 with 10 LEDs at 7 mA on 9 V: 10 × 0.007 × 7 V ≈ 0.5 W inside the chip: warm but OK.

5Series and parallel

Series (one after the other): resistances add up: R = R1 + R2. Parallel (side by side): the total is smaller than the smallest: R = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2).

Water analogy: two narrow pipes one after the other are harder to push through (series); two pipes side by side give the water two routes, so it flows more easily (parallel).

The two rules behind it (Kirchhoff)

  • Currents: what flows into a junction flows out. In parallel, the battery current splits between the branches.
  • Voltages: around a loop, the drops add up to the battery voltage. In series, the battery's 9 V is shared between the parts (e.g. 2 V + 2 V for two LEDs, 5 V left for the resistor).
  • Capacitors do the opposite: in parallel they add up (C = C1 + C2), in series they shrink.

6The voltage divider

Two resistors in series between Vin and 0 V: the middle point gives Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) (R2 is the bottom one).

It's how you make a reference voltage, read a sensor (LDR, thermistor, potentiometer) or scale a voltage down for a chip. Water analogy: two taps in a row on a sloping pipe: the pressure halfway depends on how open each one is.

⚠️ A divider is weak: if you draw current from the middle point (a load), the voltage sags. Use it to feed an input (a transistor base through a resistor, an op-amp, an Arduino pin), not to power something.

7The LED resistor

R = (Vsupply − Vled) ÷ I. The resistor takes whatever voltage the LED doesn't, and that sets the current.

LED colour:
  • Typical forward voltages: red ≈ 2 V, yellow/green ≈ 2.1 V, blue/white ≈ 3 V. 10 mA is bright enough for a 5 mm LED; 20 mA is the usual maximum.
  • Then pick the nearest standard value (E12 series: 1.0 1.2 1.5 1.8 2.2 2.7 3.3 3.9 4.7 5.6 6.8 8.2 × 10ⁿ), preferably the one just above.
  • Check the power: (Vsupply − Vled) × I. Under 0.1 W, any ¼ W resistor is fine.

8RC time and the 555

A capacitor charging through a resistor follows a curve with a "time constant" τ = R × C (seconds = ohms × farads). After 1 τ it's 63 % full, after 5 τ it's practically full.

Water analogy: filling a tank through a thin pipe: fast at first, slower and slower as the tank's back-pressure grows. A thinner pipe (bigger R) or a bigger tank (bigger C) = slower.

The 555 formulas (astable)

  • Time ON (output high): 0.693 × (R1 + R2) × C
  • Time OFF: 0.693 × R2 × C
  • Frequency: f = 1.44 ÷ ((R1 + 2 × R2) × C). Project 8: 1 kΩ, 47 kΩ, 10 µF → 1.5 Hz. Dice: same resistors, 10 nF → 1,500 Hz.
  • The flip-flop (project 16): each side ≈ 0.69 × R × C.

9Battery life

Hours ≈ capacity (mAh) ÷ current (mA). A battery's mAh is its reservoir; the current is how fast you drain it.

Battery:
  • Real life gives less, especially at high currents: count on ~70 % for a 9 V battery.
  • Energy (Wh) = V × Ah: a 3.6 V 3500 mAh 18650 cell holds 12.6 Wh, ~25 times a 9 V battery's ~0.5 Wh.
  • To save battery: higher LED resistors, "dot" mode on the LM3914, and switch off what you don't need.

1What is a sensor?

A sensor turns something from the real world (light, heat, a touch, a press, a vibrating string) into something electrical that a circuit can use: a changing resistance, a voltage, or a switch that opens and closes.

Circuits can only "see" voltages and currents. A sensor is their eye, ear or fingertip. The circuit after it then decides what to do: light a lamp, sound an alarm, show a number.

The three families

🎚️

Resistance changes

The LDR (light) and your skin (touch). They are just resistors whose value moves. Trick: put them in a voltage divider with a fixed resistor to get a voltage.
Water: a pipe that gets wider or narrower.

⚡

Voltage out

The LM35 (temperature) and the guitar pickup (vibration). They give a voltage directly: 10 mV per °C, or a few hundred mV of music.
Water: a pump whose pressure follows the world.

🔘

On / off

The push button. Either connected or not. Trick: a pull-up or pull-down resistor so the "off" state is a clean 0 V or 5 V, never floating.
Water: a tap, open or closed.

The voltage divider: the sensor's best friend

Two resistors in series between + and −: the middle point sits at a voltage set by their ratio. Replace one by a sensor and the middle voltage follows the sensor: Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2). That's how the night-light, the touch switch and most simple sensor circuits work.

Each sensor page has: what it is, how it works inside, a live demo, how to connect it, and the projects on the Circuits page that use it.

2LDR: the light sensor

An LDR (light-dependent resistor, or photoresistor) is a resistor whose value drops when light hits it: about 1 MΩ in the dark, a few kΩ in a lit room, a few hundred Ω in sunlight.

What it's for: night lights and street lamps that switch on at dusk, light meters, "is the box open?" alarms, robots that follow light. Why use one: it's cheap, needs no power of its own and works with any voltage.

How it works inside: the squiggly track is cadmium sulphide, a material whose electrons are stuck in place in the dark. Each particle of light (photon) that hits it knocks electrons loose, and free electrons can carry current. More light → more free electrons → lower resistance. It reacts in a few tens of milliseconds: fine for daylight, too slow for fast signals.

Water analogy: a pipe stuffed with ice. Sunlight melts the ice, so the brighter it is, the more water gets through.

Left: light falling on the LDR. Middle: the voltage divider used in the night light (LDR on top, 10 kΩ to ground): more light → lower LDR resistance → higher voltage. Right: resistance against light (both scales logarithmic).

How to connect it

  • Always in a divider with a fixed resistor about equal to the LDR's value at the light level you care about (10 kΩ for indoor light).
  • LDR to + and resistor to −: the voltage goes up with light. Swap them and it goes down with light.
  • Feed that voltage to a transistor, a comparator (LM358) or an Arduino analog pin.

Key facts

  • PGM1201 (Gotronic 03136): 4–10 kΩ at 10 lux, ~1 MΩ in the dark.
  • No polarity: either way round.
  • Slow (~20–50 ms) and not very precise: perfect for "dark or bright?", not for measuring lux accurately.

🔧 Used in: B. Light detector that turns on a lamp (LDR + LM358 comparator + relay).

3LM35: the temperature sensor

The LM35 is a small chip (3 legs, like a transistor) whose output voltage is the temperature: exactly 10 mV per °C. At 23 °C it gives 0.23 V. No formula, no calibration.

What it's for: thermometers, fan controllers, overheat alarms, fridge or aquarium monitors. Why use one: it's accurate (±0.5 °C at 25 °C), linear, and its output can go straight into an Arduino or a comparator.

How it works inside: it uses a property of transistors: the voltage needed to turn one on (~0.6 V) drops by about 2 mV for every degree. The LM35 compares two transistors running at different currents: the difference between them grows exactly in proportion to the temperature. An internal op-amp amplifies and trims it to a neat 10 mV/°C, set at the factory.

Water analogy: a thermometer that pushes a piston: every degree pushes the pressure up by exactly the same small step.

The output is a straight line: 0 V at 0 °C, 1 V at 100 °C. Below 0 °C the basic circuit just gives 0 V (it would need a negative supply to show negative temperatures).

How to connect it

  • LM35DZ (TO-92), flat side facing you: +Vs (4–30 V), Vout, GND. The ST039 module has the same 3 pins labelled Vcc, S, Gnd.
  • Read Vout with an Arduino analog pin (use the 1.1 V reference for 0.1 °C steps) or an LM3914 bar-graph driver.
  • Long wires? Add a 1 kΩ + 1 µF filter at the receiving end: the LM35 dislikes driving long, capacitive cables.

Key facts

  • Range 0–100 °C (LM35DZ), 10 mV/°C, uses only 60 µA so it barely heats itself.
  • It measures its own temperature: pinch it, and it shows your finger's temperature after a few seconds.

🔧 Used in: 20. Digital thermometer (Arduino + 7-segment) and 21. Bar-graph thermometer (3 × LM339 comparators, no Arduino).

4Touch pads: your skin as a sensor

Two bare wires and your finger. Your skin is a (very poor) conductor: touching both pads connects them through roughly 100 kΩ to 2 MΩ. That's a tiny current, but enough if you amplify it with transistors.

What it's for: touch lamps, buttons with no moving parts, "don't touch!" alarms, simple moisture detectors (water conducts too). Why use one: nothing to wear out, and it costs two wires.

How it works: the current through your skin is only a few µA, far too little for an LED. So it goes into the base of a transistor, which multiplies it by ~200, then into a second one (a Darlington pair), which multiplies it again by ~200: × 40,000 in total. A few µA become a few mA: the LED lights.

Water analogy: a trickle that opens a small valve, whose flow opens a big valve.

dry ← → damp

Hold the button: the tiny skin current (µA) is multiplied twice. Damp skin conducts better: try the slider. With only one transistor (× 200), dry skin wouldn't be enough.

How to connect it

  • Pad A to + through a 1 kΩ (protection), pad B to the base of the first transistor.
  • First transistor's emitter → second transistor's base; both collectors to the load (LED + 470 Ω).
  • Keep the pads close (a few mm apart) so one fingertip bridges them.

Key facts

  • Safe: 9 V through 100 kΩ+ of skin is a few tens of µA, you don't feel anything.
  • The same circuit detects water: put the pads in a glass and pour.
  • Fancier touch sensors (phones) measure capacitance instead of resistance.

🔧 Used in: 5. Touch switch (Darlington pair).

5Push button: the simplest sensor

A push button senses a finger press: a spring keeps two contacts apart, and pressing pushes a metal dome or blade against them. Pressed = connected (≈ 0 Ω), released = open (infinite Ω).

What it's for: every keypad, the reset and "roll" buttons of the dice, the counter reset, the relay START. Why use one: nothing is simpler or more reliable for "the user wants something".

Two catches:

  • Floating input: when released, a button connects the input to nothing. A chip input left floating picks up noise and reads random 0s and 1s. Fix: a pull-down resistor (10 kΩ to −, button to +) or a pull-up (10 kΩ to +, button to −). The Arduino has internal pull-ups (INPUT_PULLUP).
  • Bounce: the metal contacts bounce for 1–10 ms, so one press can look like 5 presses to a fast counter. Fix: a 100 nF capacitor across the button, or a short delay in code.

Water analogy: a tap. A pull-down resistor is a small drain that keeps the pipe empty when the tap is closed, so it never "half-fills" by itself.

Resistor:

Right: what a fast counter sees (zoomed on the first milliseconds). Without debounce, the contact bounces and the counter adds several counts per press. With "None", watch the input wander when the button is released.

Key facts

  • The 6 × 6 mm buttons have 4 legs in 2 connected pairs: place them across the breadboard gap and the pressed side connects the two halves.
  • Pull-up → reads 1 when released, 0 when pressed. Pull-down → the opposite.

🔧 Used in: 2. LED fade, 4. Traffic light, 7. Relay clicker, 12. Dice, 13–15. Dice without Arduino (reset pin with 10 kΩ pull-down), 19. Counter (reset).

6Guitar pickup: the vibration sensor

An electric guitar pickup is a magnet wrapped in thousands of turns of thin wire (a coil). The steel string, magnetised by the magnet, vibrates above it: the moving magnetism induces a small AC voltage in the coil. That voltage is the sound of the string.

What it's for: turning string vibrations into a signal for an amp like your Ruby, a pedal like the fuzz, or a tuner. Why: no battery, no microphone feedback, and it only hears the strings, not the room.

How it works inside: a changing magnetic field through a coil makes a voltage (Faraday's law, the same physics as the inductor and the switch-off spike). The faster the field changes, the bigger the voltage: so the pickup gives ~100–300 mV, wiggling at the string's frequency (110 Hz for the A string). Single coils have ~5,000–8,000 turns, about 6 kΩ of resistance and a few henries of inductance.

Water analogy: a paddle wheel in a pipe, turned back and forth by a pendulum swinging next to it: the water sloshes in time with the swing.

close ← → far

Pluck: the string swings over the magnet, the field through the coil changes, and the coil produces the voltage on the right. Raise the string (or lower the pickup) and the signal gets weaker: that's what adjusting pickup height on a real guitar does.

How to connect it

  • Two wires: hot (tip of the jack) and ground (sleeve). Usually through the guitar's volume and tone pots.
  • It's a weak, high-impedance signal: it likes a high-impedance input (≥ 500 kΩ–1 MΩ), like the Ruby's JFET buffer. A low-impedance input "loads" it and kills the treble.

Key facts

  • Humbuckers = two coils wired in opposite directions: the string signal adds up, the 50 Hz mains hum cancels.
  • Pickups only work with steel strings (nylon strings aren't magnetic).

🔧 Used in: 18. Fuzz pedal (guitar IN) and the 10. Mini amp.

7Infrared (IR) sensors

Infrared is light just beyond red (850–950 nm): invisible to your eyes, but easy to make with an IR LED and to detect with a photodiode or phototransistor. Three very different IR sensors are worth knowing: obstacle/reflection, remote-control receiver and PIR motion.

💡 Tip: your phone's camera can often see IR. Point a TV remote at it and press a button: you'll see the LED flash.

🔦

Obstacle / reflection

An IR LED shines, a phototransistor next to it looks. A nearby object (or a white surface) reflects the light back: the phototransistor conducts. A dark surface absorbs it.
Parts: TCRT5000 (2–8 mm, line-following robots), obstacle modules with a 555 and two trimmers (2–40 cm, e.g. Gotronic GT1081).
Wiring: IR LED + 220 Ω at 5 V; phototransistor collector to + through 10 kΩ, emitter to −: the collector voltage drops when something is close. Modules give a ready digital output (often LOW = obstacle).
Water: a torch and a mirror: you only see light back when something is in front.

📺

Remote-control receiver (TSOP38238)

A remote doesn't just switch its IR LED on: it flashes it 38,000 times a second in bursts. The receiver contains a photodiode, an amplifier, a 38 kHz filter and a demodulator, so it ignores sunlight and lamps and outputs clean pulses (LOW during a burst). The pattern of long and short bursts is the button code (NEC, RC5…).
Parts: TSOP38238 (Gotronic 03417, €0.95): pins OUT, GND, VS (check the datasheet: other TSOP series have another order!).
Wiring: VS to 5 V, GND, OUT to an Arduino pin with the IRremote library. Quick test without code: an LED + 1 kΩ from +5 V into OUT flickers when you press any remote button.
Water: a whistle at one exact pitch that only one dog in the park responds to.

🚶

PIR motion detector

"Passive infrared": it sends nothing. A pyroelectric crystal reacts to changes in the heat (IR) it sees. Behind the white Fresnel lens (the faceted dome), the view is split into zones: a warm body moving from zone to zone makes the signal swing, and the module's chip turns that into a clean HIGH output for a few seconds.
Parts: HC-SR501-type modules (Gotronic SBC-PIR, 3–6 m, output HIGH for 3–5 s). Two trimmers: sensitivity (range) and hold time; a jumper chooses single or repeat trigger.
Wiring: VCC 5 V, GND, OUT (3.3 V high) to an Arduino pin, or through 1 kΩ to a BC547 driving an LED, buzzer or relay. Allow ~1 minute to settle after power-up.
Water: a guard who doesn't see shapes, only notices when something warm moves between the slats of a fence.

Key facts

  • IR LEDs look like normal LEDs (often clear or bluish) and need a resistor like any LED (~1.2–1.5 V drop).
  • Sunlight is full of IR: reflection sensors work best indoors or with modulated light (like the 38 kHz trick).
  • A PIR can't see through glass (glass blocks far-infrared) and doesn't detect a person who stays perfectly still.

🔧 Ideas: a PIR night light (replace the LDR in project B by a PIR module), a remote-controlled relay with the Arduino, an obstacle-avoiding robot.

8Recap

  • A sensor turns the real world into a resistance, a voltage or an on/off contact.
  • LDR: resistance drops with light. Use it in a voltage divider.
  • LM35: 10 mV per °C, ready to measure.
  • Touch pads: your skin is a big resistor; amplify the µA with a Darlington pair.
  • Push button: always add a pull-up or pull-down, and beware of bounce.
  • Guitar pickup: a magnet + coil that turns string vibrations into a small AC voltage.
  • Infrared: reflection sensors (IR LED + phototransistor), 38 kHz remote receivers, and PIR modules that see warm bodies moving.

1What is a microchip?

A microchip (an integrated circuit, or IC) is a whole circuit, made of transistors, resistors, diodes and sometimes capacitors, built onto a tiny slice of silicon and sealed in a plastic body with metal legs (pins).

Instead of soldering 30 parts to make a timer or an amplifier, you use one chip that already contains them. Everything you learned on the transistor, resistor and diode pages is inside, just microscopic.

  • Finding pin 1: look for the notch or dot. Pin 1 is next to it, and the pins are numbered anticlockwise (seen from above): down the left side, then up the right side.

  • 100 nF beside the power pin: the decoupling capacitor from the capacitor page (example D).

4NE555 timer: the blinker

What it does: it turns on and off by itself at a rate you choose with two resistors and a capacitor. Made in 1972, it's still produced by the billions.

How it works inside: a capacitor fills through R1 + R2. Two comparators (voltage watchers) watch it: when it reaches ⅔ of the supply, the chip flips its output off and opens a discharge transistor that drains the capacitor through R2. When it falls to ⅓, the output flips back on and filling starts again.

Water analogy: a tank with a float. When the water reaches the high mark, the float opens a drain. When it drops to the low mark, the drain closes and the tank refills. The light is on while the tank is filling.

Frequency = 1.44 ÷ ((R1 + 2×R2) × C). Pick 10 nF and the blinking becomes an audible tone: that's how cheap buzzers, sirens and the classic "Atari Punk Console" synth work.

What it does: you feed it anything from about 7 V to 20 V, and it always gives exactly 5 V out, even as the battery drains or the load changes. (Its cousins: 7809 → 9 V, 7812 → 12 V, and the LM317, which is adjustable.)

How it works inside: a big transistor acts as a valve between input and output. A reference (a precise Zener-like voltage) and a comparator constantly check the output: too high, close the valve a bit; too low, open it. The extra voltage is burned off as heat: power = (Vin − 5 V) × current.

Water analogy: a pressure-reducing valve on a house's water supply. Whatever the street pressure, the taps get the same gentle pressure, and the valve gets warm doing it.

6LM358 op-amp: the comparer and amplifier

What it does: an op-amp (operational amplifier) has two inputs, + and −, and one output. It looks at the difference between the inputs and multiplies it by a huge number (about 100,000). The LM358 has two op-amps in one chip.

Two ways to use it:

  • Comparator (no feedback): the huge gain means the output simply slams to high if + is above −, or low if + is below −. It makes a yes/no decision: "is it dark?", "is it too hot?"
  • Amplifier (with feedback): two resistors feed part of the output back to the − input. The op-amp adjusts its output until both inputs are equal, which gives an exact, stable gain = 1 + Rf ÷ Rg.

Water analogy: a super-sensitive see-saw (the two inputs) connected to a powerful pump (the output). The slightest tilt drives the pump fully one way. With feedback, the pump's own pressure pushes the see-saw back level, so it settles at an exact amount.

7LM339 quad comparator: four yes/no questions

What it does: four comparators in one chip. Each one compares two voltages and answers one question: "is the − input higher than the + input?" If yes, its output connects to ground. If no, its output is simply disconnected (it "floats"). It runs from one supply, 2 to 36 V, and its inputs work right down to 0 V.

How it works inside: each comparator is a high-gain amplifier (like an op-amp) with no feedback: the tiniest difference between the inputs, a few millivolts, swings it fully one way or the other. Its output stage is a single transistor to ground, called an open collector. That is why every output needs a pull-up resistor to + (to give a clean "high" when the transistor is off), or an LED + resistor coming from + into the pin (it lights when the transistor switches on).

Water analogy: four lock-keepers, each watching two water levels. When the level on his − side gets higher than on his + side, he opens a drain to the ground. He never pumps water in, he can only drain it.

Move the signal (on −) past the threshold (on +). Then switch the pull-up off: without it, a "no" answer leaves the output floating and nothing defines the voltage.

12CD4518 dual counter: counting in decimal

What it does: it contains two counters, A and B. Each one counts clock pulses from 0 to 9 and then starts again at 0, giving the count in BCD on 4 outputs (Q1 = 1, Q2 = 2, Q3 = 4, Q4 = 8): exactly what a CD4511 wants. Chain the two halves and you count from 00 to 99.

How it works inside: each counter is 4 flip-flops (1-bit memories) that count in binary, plus a little logic that jumps from 1001 (9) straight back to 0000 instead of going on to 10–15. That second way is the trick for chaining: the units' Q4 (the "8") drops when going 9 → 0, so wired to the tens' EN it adds one ten. RESET (high) sends it back to 0.

Water analogy: a row of buckets that each hold 9 cups. Every tick pours one cup into the first bucket. When it gets its 10th cup, it tips over, empties itself, and pours one cup into the next bucket: the units carry over into the tens.

13CD4013 dual D flip-flop: a 1-bit memory with a camera shutter

What it does: two D flip-flops. Each one remembers one bit. At the exact moment its clock rises (low → high), it copies its D input to its Q output, and keeps it there until the next rising edge, whatever D does in between. Q̅ is always the opposite of Q. Two extra pins force it: S (set, Q = 1) and R (reset, Q = 0); keep them low for normal use.

The divide-by-2 trick: wire Q̅ back to D. Now D always asks for "the opposite of what Q is now", so every clock edge flips Q: 0, 1, 0, 1… Q changes once per clock pulse, so it makes one full cycle every two clock pulses: half the frequency. That's how project 26 turns the CD4060's 2 Hz into exactly 1 Hz. The CD4060 and CD4518 are built from exactly these flip-flops.

Water analogy: a camera pointed at a tap. D is the tap (open or closed); the clock is the shutter button. The photo (Q) shows the tap as it was at the click, and stays on the screen until the next click. In toggle mode it is a ballpoint pen: each click swaps in ↔ out.

Change D, then send clock pulses: Q only changes on the rising edge. Tick "toggle" to wire Q̅ → D and watch Q run at half the clock speed.

Other uses: a push-on / push-off switch (one button press = one clock; you need a debounced button, because a raw button bounces and gives several clocks), storing a sensor state at a precise moment, or a 2-bit counter with both halves chained.

14CD4060 oscillator + 14-stage divider: quartz time

What it does: two things in one chip. An oscillator (pins 9–11) that makes a fast clock with a quartz crystal, or with a resistor and a capacitor. And a chain of 14 flip-flops, each one halving the frequency of the one before (like the CD4013 in toggle mode). Ten of those stages come out on pins: Q4 to Q10 and Q12 to Q14.

How it works inside: the oscillator is an inverter (a NOT gate): its output is always the opposite of its input. Put a quartz from output to input and it keeps "correcting" itself back and forth, at the one frequency the quartz accepts. Every output Qn gives the oscillator frequency divided by 2ⁿ. With a 32.768 kHz watch quartz: Q4 = 2,048 Hz (a tone), Q10 = 32 Hz, Q14 = exactly 2 Hz. Add one more ÷2 (a CD4013) and you have 1 Hz: a clock's second.

Water analogy: a pendulum clock. The quartz is the pendulum, the inverter is the little kick that keeps it swinging, and the 14 stages are the gears that turn its fast ticks into seconds.

Fourteen stages, each half as fast as the one before. The first ones are far too fast for your eyes (they look half lit); watch the last ones blink. The grey stages exist inside but have no pin.

🔷 What is a quartz crystal, and why is it so precise?

A quartz is a thin slice of quartz crystal (the mineral, silicon dioxide) with a metal film on each side. Quartz is piezoelectric: squeeze it and it makes a small voltage; put a voltage on it and it bends a tiny bit. The same effect makes a piezo buzzer click (see Sound & motion).

The slice is cut so that it rings, mechanically, at one very exact frequency, like a tuning fork. A watch quartz is literally a tiny tuning fork inside the little metal cylinder. Electrically, it lets through almost nothing except that one frequency. So the inverter around it can only oscillate there: the quartz decides the rhythm, the chip just keeps it going.

  • Why 32,768 Hz? It's 2¹⁵: divide by 2 fifteen times and you get exactly 1 Hz. It's also slow enough for a tiny, very low-power watch circuit.
  • Precision: about ±20 ppm (parts per million): less than 2 seconds a day. A 555 with a resistor and a capacitor is off by 5–10 % and drifts with temperature and battery voltage: minutes a day.
  • The parts around it: the 10 MΩ biases the inverter to its middle point so it amplifies (it can't start otherwise). The two 22 pF capacitors are the "load" the quartz is tuned for. The 100 kΩ limits how hard the inverter shakes the tiny crystal: too much drive can damage a watch quartz.
  • Elsewhere: the Arduino's 16 MHz crystal (ATmega328P), every computer, phone, radio and watch. Faster crystals are thicker slices, not forks.

Push the quartz at different frequencies. Away from its own frequency, even by a few hertz, it barely moves. That razor-sharp response is why it's so precise.

Used in project 26: quartz stopwatch. Keep the quartz legs short and don't touch it while it runs: your finger adds capacitance and can stop it.

1574HC595 shift register: 8 outputs from 3 wires

16L293D H-bridge: a motor forwards and backwards

What it does: it drives two DC motors in both directions, from logic signals (buttons, an Arduino). Up to 600 mA per motor (1.2 A peaks), with a motor supply from 4.5 to 36 V on pin 8 and a separate logic supply on pin 16. How it works inside: the H-bridge. To reverse a DC motor, you reverse the current through it. An H-bridge is four switches arranged like the letter H, with the motor as the middle bar. Close top-left and bottom-right: current flows left → right. Close the other diagonal: right → left. The L293D has four "half-bridges" (1Y, 2Y, 3Y, 4Y): each output is switched either to + or to ground, copying its input. Two outputs make one H-bridge. Because each output is either + or −, never both, you can't make the deadly short circuit of closing both switches on one side.

Water analogy: a water wheel in a pipe between two tanks. Raise the left tank and lower the right: it turns one way. Swap them: it turns the other way. Same level on both sides: it stops. The L293D is the one lifting the tanks.

Water analogy: filling a bottle from a tap: full flow at first, then you slow down as you approach the brim, and stop exactly at the line. Over-filling a Li-ion cell (above ~4.25 V) is dangerous, hence the precise chip.

18ATmega328P: the brain of the Arduino

What it does: it's a complete little computer on one chip: a processor, 32 KB of memory for your program, 2 KB of working memory, and 23 input/output pins that your code can switch, read, time and measure. 13Recap: the method works for any part

  • Identify what it really is: a 7-segment display is 8 LEDs with a common pin; a bar graph is 10 fully separate LEDs.
  • Limits: forward voltage, current, reverse voltage. Stay well below the maximums.
  • Pinout and "common": common cathode → COM to ground; no common pin → each LED has its own pair (bar graph: anode n ↔ cathode 21 − n). Confirm with a quick test.
  • Support parts: one resistor per LED (or a network, or a current-regulating driver like the LM3914), and a decoder, comparators or a microcontroller to drive it.
  • Physical fit: 2.54 mm pitch fits a breadboard.

1LED brightness with different resistors

Resistor See how the resistor sets the current, and so the brightness. 📖 lesson

🧰 You need

  • 9 V battery + clip
  • 1 red LED (5 mm)
  • Resistors: 220 Ω, 470 Ω, 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ
  • Breadboard + jumper wires

💡 How it works

The battery pushes current around one single loop: + → resistor → LED → −. The resistor is the narrow pipe that sets how much current can flow, and the LED glows brighter with more current.

The red LED always "uses" about 2 V of the 9 V. The other 7 V is dropped across the resistor, so the current is I = 7 V ÷ R: 220 Ω gives 32 mA (too much!), 470 Ω gives 15 mA, 1 kΩ gives 7 mA, and 10 kΩ gives 0.7 mA (a faint glow).

Series resistors add up (harder to flow, dimmer). Parallel resistors give the current two paths (easier, brighter).

2LED that fades out slowly

Capacitor Watch a capacitor act as an energy reserve. 📖 lesson

🧰 You need

  • 9 V battery
  • Push button
  • Electrolytic capacitors: 100 µF, 470 µF, 1000 µF (16 V or more)
  • 470 Ω resistor, red LED

💡 How it works

While you hold the button, the battery feeds two things at once: the LED (through its resistor) and the big capacitor, which fills up to 9 V almost instantly.

When you let go, the battery is disconnected. The capacitor now acts like a small water tank: it empties through the resistor and the LED, so the LED keeps glowing.

As the capacitor empties, its voltage drops, the current drops, and the LED fades. A bigger capacitor (more water) or a bigger resistor (narrower pipe) makes the fade last longer: roughly time ≈ R × C.

5Touch switch (skin turns on an LED)

Transistor Two transistors amplify the tiny current through your finger about 40,000×. 📖 lesson

🧰 You need

  • 9 V battery
  • 2 × BC547 (NPN)
  • 1 kΩ + 470 Ω resistors
  • LED, 2 bare wires as touch pads

💡 How it works

Your skin conducts a tiny current (a few µA) between the two pads. That's far too little to light an LED.

Transistor 1 amplifies it about 200×, and its output feeds the base of transistor 2, which amplifies it about 200× again. Together (a Darlington pair) that's ×40,000: a few µA through your finger become a few mA through the LED.

The 1 kΩ protects the base if the pads touch each other directly, and the 470 Ω limits the LED current.

🤔 Why two transistors? Isn't one enough?

A transistor is not a light switch, it's a multiplier. When it's "on", it doesn't let everything through: it lets through at most its gain × the current going into its base. For a BC547 the gain is about 200.

Your finger gives almost nothing. Skin is a poor conductor (about 500 kΩ, more when dry), so only about 17 µA (millionths of an amp) flows through it. With one transistor: 200 × 17 µA = 3.4 mA at most. The LED wants about 14 mA to shine properly, so it only glows faintly. With dry skin (~4 µA) it's 0.8 mA: almost nothing.

Two transistors multiply twice. The first one's output becomes the second one's input: 200 × 200 = 40,000. Now 17 µA could become 680 mA, far more than needed, so the second transistor opens fully and the 470 Ω resistor sets a nice 14 mA. Even dry fingers work.

💧 Water analogy: your finger is a tiny trickle. It can only open a small valve a little. Put that small valve's flow on a big valve, and the big valve opens wide.

wet ← → dry

Compare the "available" current with what the LED needs. With one transistor and a dry finger, the LED barely glows. Tip: click the transistors in the schematic above to see the real base and collector currents with the probe.

🔗 Why are the two collectors joined (and not on +9 V)?

Safety. If the first collector went straight to +9 V and the pads got shorted (a coin, a stray wire), about 8 mA would enter transistor 1. It would then try to pass 200 × 8 mA = 1.6 A straight into the base of transistor 2, with nothing to stop it. Both transistors would burn.

Joined after the LED, everything transistor 1 passes must first go through the 470 Ω and the LED. The total is capped at ~14 mA, whatever happens at the pads. Bonus: transistor 1's small current also lights the LED.

💧 Water analogy: the 470 Ω is a narrow section at the entrance. Both valves drink from after it, so neither can ever get a flood.

Small cost: the pair keeps ~0.9 V across itself instead of 0.2 V, so the LED gets ~13 mA instead of 14. Ready-made Darlingtons like the TIP120 are wired exactly this way inside.

16Two-LED flip-flop (no chip!)

Transistors only Two LEDs that blink in turn, made from just 2 transistors, 2 capacitors and 4 resistors. No chip at all: this is the circuit hidden inside the 555 and every blinker, built from loose parts so you can see how an oscillator works.

Left: the circuit, with each capacitor's charge. Right: the voltage on each transistor's base. It dives below 0 V when the other transistor switches on, then climbs back up through 47 kΩ; at 0.6 V the transistor switches on and the LEDs swap. Try different C1 and C2.

🧰 You need

  • 2 × BC547
  • 2 × 10 µF electrolytic capacitors
  • 2 × 47 kΩ, 2 × 470 Ω
  • 2 LEDs (one red, one green)
  • 9 V battery + clip, breadboard + jumpers

💡 How it works

Two transistors, each one able to switch the other off for a moment. When Q1 turns on, its collector drops from ~9 V to ~0 V. The capacitor linking it to Q2's base passes that sudden drop on, and pushes Q2's base below 0 V: Q2 is slammed off.

Then Q2's base slowly climbs back up as its 47 kΩ resistor refills the capacitor. When it reaches ~0.6 V, Q2 turns on, its collector drops, and the other capacitor slams Q1 off. And so on, forever: a see-saw that tips itself.

Water analogy: two buckets on a see-saw, each slowly filled by a thin pipe (the 47 kΩ). When one is full it tips the see-saw, which empties the other bucket. Time per side ≈ 0.7 × R × C = 0.7 × 47 kΩ × 10 µF ≈ 0.33 s.

This is exactly the job a 555 does (project 8), but built from loose parts. It's also the oldest oscillator in electronics: the "astable multivibrator" from 1917, then with vacuum tubes.

8555 blinker (and buzzer)

Microchips The classic first chip project. 📖 lesson

🧰 You need

  • NE555, 9 V battery
  • R1 1 kΩ, R2 10–100 kΩ
  • C 10 µF
  • LED + 470 Ω
  • (variant) 10 nF capacitor + small speaker or piezo

💡 How it works

The 10 µF capacitor fills through R1 + R2. Inside the 555, two comparators watch its voltage.

When it reaches ⅔ of 9 V, the 555 turns its output (pin 3) off and opens the discharge pin 7, which empties the capacitor through R2. When it falls to ⅓, the output turns back on and filling starts again.

That endless fill/empty cycle makes the LED blink. Frequency ≈ 1.44 ÷ ((R1 + 2·R2) × C): with 1 kΩ, 47 kΩ and 10 µF, about 1.5 blinks per second.

💡 How it works

A quartz is a tiny slice of crystal that vibrates at one single frequency, like a tuning fork. A watch quartz vibrates at 32,768 times per second. Inside the CD4060, an inverter (a NOT gate) keeps it vibrating: the 10 MΩ biases it, the two 22 pF and the 100 kΩ make the crystal the only thing that sets the rhythm.

Why 32,768? Because it's 2¹⁵: divide it by 2 fifteen times and you get exactly 1 Hz. The 1 Hz goes into the counter of project 19 instead of the 555. The NE555's rhythm depends on a resistor and a capacitor (5–10 % tolerance, and it drifts with temperature and battery). The quartz is accurate to about ±20 ppm: less than 2 seconds per day.

// Digital dice: 2 × Kingbright SC39-11SURKWA (common cathode), multiplexed
const byte SEG[8]   = {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9};  // a b c d e f g dp (each via 330 Ω)
const byte DIGIT[2] = {10, 11};                  // → 1 kΩ → BC547 base, collector = display COM
const byte BUTTON   = 12;                        // push button to GND
const byte FONT[10] = {0x3F,0x06,0x5B,0x4F,0x66,0x6D,0x7D,0x07,0x7F,0x6F}; // bit0 = a … bit6 = g
byte value[2] = {6, 6};

void showDigit(byte d, byte n) {
  for (byte s = 0; s < 8; s++) digitalWrite(SEG[s], bitRead(FONT[n], s));
  digitalWrite(DIGIT[d], HIGH);    // transistor ON → this display lights
  delay(4);
  digitalWrite(DIGIT[d], LOW);     // OFF before changing the segments (no "ghosting")
}

void refresh(unsigned long ms) {   // keep both displays lit for ms milliseconds
  unsigned long start = millis();
  while (millis() - start < ms) { showDigit(0, value[0]); showDigit(1, value[1]); }
}

void setup() {
  for (byte s = 0; s < 8; s++) pinMode(SEG[s], OUTPUT);
  for (byte d = 0; d < 2; d++) pinMode(DIGIT[d], OUTPUT);
  pinMode(BUTTON, INPUT_PULLUP);
  randomSeed(analogRead(A0));      // unconnected pin = random noise
}

void loop() {
  if (digitalRead(BUTTON) == LOW) {                 // button pressed: roll!
    for (int wait = 20; wait < 260; wait += 15) {   // spins fast, then slows down
      value[0] = random(1, 7);                      // 1 … 6
      value[1] = random(1, 7);
      refresh(wait);
    }
  }
  refresh(20);
}

🧰 You need

  • 2 × Kingbright SC39-11SURKWA (Gotronic 03099)
  • Arduino Uno or compatible (e.g. Joy-It ONE-C)
  • 8 × 330 Ω (segments), 2 × 1 kΩ (bases)
  • 2 × BC547B transistors
  • 1 push button, breadboard + jumper wires

💡 How it works

The two displays share the same 8 segment wires, so the Arduino can't light them both with different numbers at the same moment. Instead it shows display 1 for 4 ms, then display 2 for 4 ms, again and again. Your eye blends it into two steady numbers: multiplexing.

Each display's COM (the shared cathode) goes to ground through its own BC547. The Arduino turns one transistor on at a time (D10 or D11). The transistors carry the whole digit's current (up to 8 × 9 mA), which a single Arduino pin couldn't.

When you press the button (D12, internal pull-up so it reads LOW when pressed), the code picks random numbers faster at first, then slower, until it stops: like a real die rolling to a stop.

Why not a ready-made counter that counts 1–6 in binary? The ideal chip (CD4510, a presettable BCD counter) isn't sold at Gotronic, and the CD4518 can only reset to 0. Using the 4017 + a diode encoder is a neat workaround with parts you already have.

// Digital thermometer: LM35 module + 2 × SC39-11SURKWA (same wiring as the dice, project 12)
const byte SEG[8]   = {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9};  // a b c d e f g dp (each via 330 Ω)
const byte DIGIT[2] = {10, 11};                  // → 1 kΩ → BC547 base, collector = display COM
const byte SENSOR   = A0;                        // LM35 module "S" pin: 10 mV per °C
const byte FONT[11] = {0x3F,0x06,0x5B,0x4F,0x66,0x6D,0x7D,0x07,0x7F,0x6F, 0x00}; // 0–9, 10 = blank
byte value[2] = {10, 0};

void showDigit(byte d, byte n) {
  for (byte s = 0; s < 8; s++) digitalWrite(SEG[s], bitRead(FONT[n], s));
  digitalWrite(DIGIT[d], HIGH);   // transistor ON → this display lights
  delay(4);
  digitalWrite(DIGIT[d], LOW);    // OFF before changing the segments
}

void refresh(unsigned long ms) {  // keep both displays lit for ms milliseconds
  unsigned long start = millis();
  while (millis() - start < ms) { showDigit(0, value[0]); showDigit(1, value[1]); }
}

float readTemp() {
  long sum = 0;
  for (int i = 0; i < 20; i++) sum += analogRead(SENSOR);  // average 20 readings: steadier
  float volts = sum / 20.0 * 1.1 / 1023.0;                 // 1.1 V internal reference
  return volts * 100.0;                                    // 10 mV per °C → °C
}

void setup() {
  for (byte s = 0; s < 8; s++) pinMode(SEG[s], OUTPUT);
  for (byte d = 0; d < 2; d++) pinMode(DIGIT[d], OUTPUT);
  analogReference(INTERNAL);      // measure 0–1.1 V instead of 0–5 V: 0.1 °C steps
  analogRead(SENSOR);             // the first reading after switching is junk
}

void loop() {
  static unsigned long last = 0;
  if (millis() - last > 500) {    // a new measurement twice per second
    last = millis();
    int t = constrain((int)(readTemp() + 0.5), 0, 99);   // round to whole degrees
    value[0] = (t < 10) ? 10 : t / 10;                    // tens (blank if 0)
    value[1] = t % 10;                                     // units
  }
  refresh(20);
}

🧰 You need

  • LM35 temperature module ST039 (Gotronic 35208)

💡 How it works

The LM35 is a chip that gives a voltage proportional to temperature: exactly 10 mV per °C. At 23 °C its S pin sits at 0.23 V. The module just puts it on a small board with 3 pins.

The Arduino's A0 pin contains an analog-to-digital converter (ADC): it compares the voltage with a reference and gives a number from 0 to 1023. With the normal 5 V reference, one step is 4.9 mV, so ~0.5 °C. The code switches to the chip's internal 1.1 V reference: one step is 1.07 mV, about 0.1 °C, and 0–110 °C still fits.

Water analogy: the ADC is a measuring jug with 1024 marks. A 5 V jug for a few drops of water (0.23 V) is hard to read; the small 1.1 V jug has its marks much closer together.

Then it's the same as the dice: 20 readings are averaged (less jitter), converted to °C, rounded, split into tens and units, and shown on the two displays with multiplexing (each one lit 4 ms at a time, too fast for your eye).

🧰 You need

💡 How it works

The LM35 gives 10 mV per °C (0.22 V at 22 °C). Each comparator in the LM339 answers one question: "is the temperature signal (−) higher than my threshold (+)?". If yes, its output connects to ground and its LED lights. Three LM339 = 12 comparators; we use 10.

The ladder makes the 10 thresholds: a small current (0.2 mA) flows from +5 V through 20 kΩ + the trimmer, then nine 100 Ω, then 800 Ω to ground. Each 100 Ω drops 0.2 mA × 100 Ω = 20 mV = 2 °C. The 800 Ω at the bottom lifts the first step to 160 mV = 16 °C. The trimmer adjusts the current, so it stretches or shrinks the whole scale.

Warmer → higher signal → it passes more rungs → more comparators say "yes" → a longer bar. This is exactly what is inside an LM3914, built by hand. The difference: the LM339 doesn't limit the LED current, so each LED gets its own 470 Ω (≈ 6 mA). Eight of them come in one resistor network: 8 resistors in one flat package, all joined on one side (pin 1, marked with a dot), which goes to +5 V.

💧 Water analogy: a tall tank with 10 overflow holes at equal heights (the ladder), each feeding a little light. The warmer it is, the higher the water rises, and the more holes spill over.

Right at a threshold, the signal wobbles by a fraction of a mV and the last segment may flicker. That's normal: a real product adds a little hysteresis (a 1 MΩ from each output back to its + input).

🧰 You need

💡 How it works

The problem: everything glows in infrared: the sun, lamps, radiators, your body. A plain IR sensor would be fooled all the time. The trick: the IR LED blinks 38,000 times per second, and the TSOP contains a filter that only reacts to light blinking at exactly that speed. Like hearing your own name in a noisy room: everything else is ignored. TV remotes use the same trick.

555 #2 makes the 38 kHz (1 kΩ, 3.53 kΩ and 4.7 nF). 555 #1 switches it on and off about 690 times per second, in bursts, through 555 #2's reset pin. Why? The TSOP slowly gets used to a signal that never stops (it treats it as noise) and goes deaf. Bursts keep it listening.

The TSOP's OUT goes low during each burst it receives. The diode lets it empty the 10 µF "bucket" at every burst, while the 4.7 kΩ slowly refills it. Beam present: the bucket is emptied 690 times a second and stays near 0.8 V. Beam blocked: nothing empties it, it fills to ~3.6 V in a few hundredths of a second, the BC547 turns on and the red LED lights.

💧 Water analogy: a bucket under a small tap (4.7 kΩ), with a drain that opens at every burst (the TSOP). As long as the bursts arrive, the bucket stays empty. Block the beam: the drain stays shut, the bucket fills up and overflows into the alarm.

The two 10 kΩ resistors form a divider: the base only gets enough voltage when the bucket is above ~1.3 V. That stops the 0.8 V "empty" level from half-lighting the LED. See the infrared sensors page for more on the TSOP.

23Motor speed control (PWM)

Motors Turn a knob, the motor goes from crawling to full speed. A 555 switches the motor on and off 13,000 times per second, and the knob sets how long each "on" lasts. That trick (PWM) is how almost every modern device controls motors, fans and LED brightness.

Left: the 555 output, zoomed in (one period = 76 µs). The motor only feels the average (dashed line). Right: the motor speed follows that average. Below ~15 %, the motor is too weak to start.

🧰 You need

  • DC motor MFA RE140/1, 3–9 V (Gotronic 24851)
  • SS8050 NPN transistor, 1.5 A (Gotronic 43143)
  • NE555, 10 kΩ trimmer, 2 × 1N4148, 1N4007
  • 2 × 1 kΩ, 470 Ω, red LED, 10 nF, 100 µF

💡 How it works

A motor's speed depends on the average voltage it gets. We could waste the extra voltage in a big resistor (it would get hot), but there's a smarter trick: switch the full 9 V on and off very fast, about 13,000 times per second. That's PWM (pulse-width modulation). On 30 % of the time = like 30 % of 9 V ≈ 2.7 V on average. The motor is far too heavy to follow each pulse: it just feels the average.

The 555 makes the pulses. The trimmer splits its 10 kΩ in two: the capacitor charges through one side and discharges through the other (each diode lets only one direction through). Turn the knob: one side grows as the other shrinks, so the on-time changes but the total period stays the same.

The 555 can't feed a motor (it would need ~200 mA, more when starting). So it drives a transistor, like in the relay clicker. But the BC547 only takes 100 mA, so we use an SS8050: same job, up to 1.5 A. The 1N4007 handles the coil kick, exactly like for a relay.

💧 Water analogy: instead of half-closing a tap (which wastes pressure), you open it fully and close it fully, very fast. A heavy water wheel can't follow the flicker: it turns at the average flow.

25Lower the voltage for a small motor

Motors Small toy motors are often made for 3–6 V. How do you run one from a 9 V battery? With a resistor (bad idea, see why), with diodes (cheap and good), with a capacitor (doesn't work, see why), or with PWM. Build the diode version with your RE140/1 and compare.

🧰 You need

  • 4 × 1N4007 diodes (you have 10)
  • DC motor RE140/1 (or any small 3–6 V motor from an old toy)
  • 1000 µF capacitor

💡 How it works

Why not 9 V on a small 3–6 V motor? Too much voltage = too much speed and current: the coil heats up, the brushes spark and wear out, and its life drops from hundreds of hours to a few. It won't explode, but it dies early. (The RE140/1 you're buying is rated 3–9 V, so 9 V is fine for it. This page is for smaller motors, like one from an old toy.)

A resistor? A resistor drops U = R × I, so the drop depends on the current. A motor's current changes all the time: ~1 A when starting, 0.1 A spinning freely, more when you brake it. So the voltage it gets changes too: too little exactly when it needs it most (starting, or under load), and too much when spinning freely. And it heats up: 3 V × 0.25 A = 0.75 W burns a 1/4 W resistor.

Diodes! A silicon diode drops about 0.7 V almost whatever the current (0.65 V at 10 mA, 0.85 V at 1 A). Four in series take about 3 V off: 9 V → ~6 V, steady from start-up to full load. Each 1N4007 handles 1 A. It's the cheapest "voltage reducer" for motors. (They still turn the 3 V into heat, but spread over 4 diodes it's fine.)

And the capacitor? A capacitor does not lower a voltage. It's a bucket that fills up to the same level as what feeds it: on 9 V it charges to 9 V. What it does: it smooths (fills the dips), and gives a short boost of current at start-up. With a diode in front, a diode + capacitor make a rectifier (turn AC into DC, as in a phone charger): that's probably the combination you heard about. To really control a voltage, you need either diodes (fixed drops), a zener diode + transistor, or a regulator like the 7805, which is exactly that inside a chip.

💧 Water analogy: the resistor is a narrow pipe: the more water flows, the more pressure it loses. Each diode is a small step of fixed height (0.7 V): the water always loses the same height, whatever the flow. The capacitor is a tank on the side: it can't lower the water level, it can only keep it steady.

How lithium charging works: CC then CV.

  1. Constant Current (CC): the charger pushes a fixed current (e.g. 1 A) while the battery voltage slowly rises from ~3.0 V towards 4.2 V. This fills about 70–80% of the battery.
  2. Constant Voltage (CV): at 4.2 V the charger holds the voltage exactly there, and the current fades away as the battery tops up, just like a capacitor filling.
  3. Done: when the current drops to ~10% (e.g. 100 mA), the charger stops. Going above 4.2 V damages Li-ion cells and can make them catch fire, so the chip watches it precisely.

The TP4056 chip does all of this. The cheap blue modules also include a protection chip (DW01 + a MOSFET pair) that cuts the battery off if it's over-discharged (< 2.5 V), over-charged or short-circuited.

🧰 You need

  • TP4056 charger module (Gotronic GT060)
  • BMS 1S protection board (B+, B−, P+, P−)
  • 18650 Li-ion cell (65 mm) + holder with wires
  • USB cable / 5 V phone charger
  • A switch + a load to test (LED + 100 Ω, or a 5 V boost module for a power bank)
  • Multimeter

💡 How it works

The TP4056 takes 5 V from USB and charges the cell in two phases. First constant current (1 A) while the cell voltage rises, then constant voltage (exactly 4.2 V) while the current fades. It stops at about 100 mA.

The BMS sits between the cell and everything else. It contains a tiny chip and two MOSFET switches that disconnect the cell if it's over-charged, over-discharged (below ~2.5 V) or short-circuited.

The charger and the load both connect to the BMS's P+ / P− side, so they're always protected. The cell itself only touches B+ / B−.

BReal life: a light detector that turns on a lamp

Real life A garden or hallway light that switches on by itself at dusk. It combines almost everything: a divider, an op-amp comparator, a transistor, a relay and a flyback diode.

  1. Sensor: an LDR + 10 kΩ divider. In the dark the LDR's resistance rises, so the divider voltage rises.
  2. Threshold knob: a 10 kΩ pot sets the "how dark" voltage.
  3. Decision: the LM358 compares the two. Sensor above threshold means dark, so the output goes HIGH. A 1 MΩ resistor adds hysteresis (a small dead band) so the lamp doesn't flicker at dusk.
  4. Muscle: the output → 1 kΩ → BC547 → relay coil (+ flyback diode) → its contact switches the 12 V lamp.

🧰 You need

  • LDR (photoresistor) + 10 kΩ resistor
  • 10 kΩ potentiometer (threshold)
  • LM358 + 1 MΩ (hysteresis) + 100 nF decoupling
  • BC547 + 1 kΩ + 1N4007
  • 12 V relay (or 5 V relay + 5 V supply)
  • 12 V lamp/LED strip + 12 V supply

💡 How it works

The 10 kΩ + LDR divider gives a voltage that rises as it gets darker (the LDR's resistance shoots up). The trimmer gives an adjustable threshold voltage.

The LM358 compares them. Sensor above threshold means dark, so its output jumps HIGH. The 1 MΩ feeds a little of the output back to the + input (hysteresis), so once it switches it stays switched, and the lamp doesn't flicker at dusk.

The HIGH output drives the BC547 through 1 kΩ, the transistor powers the relay coil, and the relay's contact closes the separate 12 V lamp circuit. The 1N4007 protects the transistor from the coil's switch-off spike.
